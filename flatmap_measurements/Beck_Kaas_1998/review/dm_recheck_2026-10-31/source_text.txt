Thalamic Connections of the Dorsomedial
Visual Area in Primates
PAMELA D. BECK AND JON H. KAAS*
Department of Psychology, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee 37240
ABSTRACT
The dorsomedial visual area (DM) of owl monkeys is a cortical area that has been
described recently in a range of primate species. To study the thalamic connections of this
area, injections of several distinguishable neuroanatomical tracers were placed into DM in
galagos, owl monkeys, squirrel monkeys, and macaque monkeys. The distribution of label was
remarkably consistent across these diverse primate species. Labeled connections were
densest within the pulvinar complex. Both the lateral and inferior divisions of the pulvinar,
but not the medial division, had connections with DM. Within the inferior pulvinar of
monkeys, central lateral and central medial nuclei had dense connections, and the medial and
posterior nuclei had sparse connections with DM. Sparser connections were revealed in the
lateral geniculate nucleus and the nucleus limitans. Anterograde label was also found in the
superior colliculus. The consistencies in the pattern of subcortical projections across prosim￾ian primates, New World monkeys, and Old World monkeys support the concept that DM is a
visual area common to all primates. In addition, these results provide further evidence for
proposed subdivisions of the inferior pulvinar. J. Comp. Neurol. 396:381–398, 1998.

The dorsomedial visual area (DM) is a subdivision of
extrastriate visual cortex that was first described in New
World owl monkeys (Allman and Kaas, 1975) as one of a
series of postulated visual areas forming the rostral border
of the second visual area, V2. Other early investigators,
especially those exploring visual cortex organization in
Old World macaque monkeys, did not describe a series of
visual areas along the rostral border of V2 but, rather,
proposed that much or all of this cortex was occupied by a
single visual field, the third visual area, V3 (Cragg, 1969;
Zeki, 1969). This interpretation was fortified by the results
of later electrophysiological mapping studies (Gattass et
al., 1988). However, differences in the connections and the
response properties of neurons in the dorsal and ventral
halves of the V3 region led some to consider each half as a
separate visual area (Burkhalter et al., 1986). Subsequent
magnetic resonance imaging studies of the organization of
human visual cortex have tended to portray a single
bordering area, V3 (Shipp et al., 1995), or a dorsal V3
(Sereno et al., 1995). Thus, one possibility is that the
organization of extrastriate cortex varies, with New World
monkeys and prosimians having a DM and Old World
monkeys and humans having a V3.
In owl monkeys, DM contains a retinotopic map of both
the upper and lower quadrants of the contralateral visual
hemifield, is densely myelinated, and has dense connec￾tions with V1, V2, the middle temporal visual area (MT),
and part of posterior parietal cortex (Allman and Kaas,
1975; Wagor et al., 1975; Krubitzer and Kaas, 1990;
Krubitzer and Kaas, 1993). Subcortical connections in￾clude the superior and inferior divisions of the pulvinar
complex and the superior colliculus (Norden et al., 1978).
More recently, several groups of investigators have found
some of these characteristics of DM in a region of dorsome￾dial extrastriate visual cortex of other primates, suggest￾ing that DM exists in these studied primates as well as in
owl monkeys. Most notably, a retinotopic map of the
complete contralateral hemifield in a densely myelinated
oval of cortex in the expected position of DM has now been
reported for New World marmosets (Rosa and Schmid,
1995) and prosimian galagos (Rosa et al., 1997). In addi￾tion, similar connection patterns between V1 and a densely
myelinated DM-like region of cortex have been used as
evidence for DM in galagos, marmosets, squirrel monkeys,
owl monkeys, patas monkeys, and macaque monkeys

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Connections of the pulvinar complex and other thalamic
nuclei with DM were examined by placing one to five
injections of different neuroanatomical tracers into DM in
four species of primates. Foci of label were localized within
thalamic nuclei and in subdivisions of the pulvinar com￾plex that were identified with various histochemical and
immunocytochemical stains. DM was identified as a densely
myelinated field just rostral to dorsomedial V2 and by its
dense myelination.
Animals
Three galagos (Galago garnetti), five owl monkeys (Ao￾tus trivirgatus), two squirrel monkeys (Saimiri sciureus),
and four macaque monkeys (three Macaca nemestrina and
one Macaca fasicularis) were used in this study. Three of
the five owl monkey brains with DM injections were
available from a related study of cortical connections
(Krubitzer and Kaas, 1993). This range of species includes
representatives of three major lines of primate evolution.
Galagos are nocturnal, prosimian primates that represent
the lorisiform branch of primate evolution. Both owl
monkeys and squirrel monkeys are New World monkeys,
but owl monkeys are nocturnal, and squirrel monkeys are
diurnal. Macaque monkeys are diurnal Old World mon￾keys.
Surgery and injections
All surgical procedures were performed under sterile
conditions in accordance with guidelines from the National
Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Vanderbilt Animal Care
Committee. Animals were initially anesthetized with ket￾amine hydrochloride (30 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg), or
with 3% isoflurane gas administered through a face mask.
When these animals reached a surgical level of anesthesia,
a dosage of 1.5–2.0% isoflurane was administered through
an endotracheal tube. Dexamethasone was given pre- and
postoperatively to prevent cerebral swelling. Heart rate,
temperature, and respiration were monitored throughout
surgery, and Ringers’ solution (subcutaneous) and antibiot￾ics (intramuscular) were injected postoperatively.
After the head was secured in a stereotaxic frame, an
incision was placed along the midline of the scalp, bone
was removed, and cortex in the region of dorsomedial
visual cortex was exposed. Except for macaque monkeys,
the location of DM was estimated by using distances
relative to sulci and the midline. Pressure injections
through a glass micropipette sealed to a 1 µl or 5 µl
Hamilton syringe (Reno, NV) were placed 0.8–1 mm below
the cortical surface. In macaque monkeys, a part of the
prelunate gyrus was aspirated to allow access to the
caudal branch of the lunate sulcus (see Fig. 1). Injections
were then placed in the cortex on the caudal, lower bank of
the lunate sulcus or onto the annectent gyrus ventral to a
large blood vessel that divides the upper and lower banks
of the lunate sulcus. Typically, different neuroanatomical
tracers were placed at several sites within the expected
location of DM. Injected tracers included wheat germ
agglutinin conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (WGA￾HRP; 0.02 µl of 2% WGA-HRP in saline), Fast blue (FB;
0.1–0.2 µl of 2% FB in saline), Fluororuby (FR; 0.1–0.3 µl of
10% FR in saline). Diamidino yellow (DY; 0.2–0.3 µl of 2%
DY in dH2O), Fluorescein (FS; 0.2 µl of 2% FS in saline),
and Fluoroemerald (FE; 0.2 µl of 10% FE in saline).
After the injections were completed, the exposed cortex
was covered with dura, a sterile, gas-permeable contact
lens, and Gelfoam. In addition, a small piece of Gelfoam
was placed in the lesion created by aspiration in macaque
monkeys. The craniotomy was covered by a cap of quick￾curing dental cement that was secured to the cranium with
the same material. The wound was sutured, and anesthe￾sia was discontinued and replaced with oxygen until the
animal showed signs of recovery. The endotracheal tube
was then removed, and the animal was allowed to recover
fully from anesthesia on a warming pad.

Histology
Following a survival period of 7–14 days, sufficient for
transport of the tracers, animals were initially anesthe￾tized with ketamine hydrochloride and xylazine and were
then given a lethal dose of sodium pentobarbital. When
they were areflexive, animals were perfused with physi￾ological saline, followed by 2% paraformaldehyde in phos￾phate buffer, then 2% paraformaldehyde with 10% sucrose
in phosphate buffer (0.1M), pH 7.4. The cranium was
opened, and the brain was removed. Cortex and thalamus
were separated, and the cortex was processed for a series
of related studies on cortical connections. The thalamus
was stored overnight in a solution of 30% sucrose in
phosphate buffer.
The following day, the thalamus was cut and processed
for histology. Sections were cut 50 µm thick in a coronal
plane on a freezing microtome and reserved in phosphate
buffer. Every fourth to fifth section was treated for either
tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Mesulam, 1978), cytochrome
oxidase (CO; Wong-Riley, 1979), acetylcholinesterase
(AChE; Geneser-Jensen and Blackstad, 1971), or calbindin
(CB; Celio, 1990) or was left unprocessed for fluorescence
microscopy. Sections were then mounted onto gelatin￾coated glass slides, allowed to dry, and coverslipped.
Data analysis
Brain sections were drawn showing locations of labeled
cells, terminals, and architectonic borders. The locations of
fluorescent labeling were digitized by using a fluorescent
microscope equipped with a computer-interfaced X-Y en￾coder and were then printed at a low magnification by
using the Bioquant image-analysis system (R and M
Biometrics, Nashville, TN). Label from the WGA-HRP
injection and borders of thalamic nuclei were recon￾structed at the same magnification by using a drawing
tube attached to a lightfield/darkfield microscope.
RESULTS
In this report, we describe the subcortical projections of
the visual area DM (or the presumptive DM) in four
taxonomic groups of primates. The locations and borders of
DM shown for galagos, owl monkeys, squirrel monkeys,
and macaque monkeys in Figure 1 were determined in the
present study from the myeloarchitecture of dorsomedial
visual cortex studied in brain sections cut parallel to the
surface of manually flattened cortex. In all of these pri￾mates, DM was defined as a darkly myelinated region
along the rostral border of V2. The myeloarchitectonic
borders of DM in owl monkeys, squirrel monkeys, galagos,
macaques, and other monkeys have also been demon￾strated repeatedly elsewhere (Allman and Kaas, 1975;
Wagor et al., 1975; Krubitzer and Kaas, 1990, 1993; Rosa
and Schmid, 1995; Stepniewska and Kaas, 1996). In the
present cases, the border with V2 was most obvious due to
the characteristic banding pattern of V2 (Livingstone and
Hubel, 1982; Stepniewska and Kaas, 1997). The precise
locations of other borders were less certain, but the general
extent of DM was clear. Histological documentation on the
proposed borders of DM will be presented elsewhere with
our reports on cortical connections. The location of DM in
the present cases closely corresponds to the region defined
as DM in previous studies (see Discussion). All injections
judged to be within DM in the present cases labeled
neurons in V1 and V2, structures that are known to project
to DM (Krubitzer and Kaas, 1993; Stepniewska and Kaas,
1996). Here, we describe the subcortical connections of DM
with the pulvinar complex, the dorsal lateral geniculate
nucleus (LGN), the nucleus limitans, and the superior
colliculus. Other subcortical connections with the thalamic
reticular nucleus, the claustrum, and the caudate nucleus
were observed, but they are not described here.
Galagos
The subcortical connections were studied after a total of
eight distinguishable injections or sets of injections into
DM of three galagos. In one animal, injections of four
different fluorescent tracers were made into the caudal
half of DM (Fig. 3). In the second animal, one large
injection of WGA-HRP was placed in central-medial DM,
and a smaller and more restricted DY injection was placed
more caudally (Figs. 4, 5). In the third animal, one large
injection of FB was placed in central-medial DM, and a
smaller DY injection was made more caudally (not shown).
Diffusion from the WGA-HRP injection of the second
animal (case 95–09) involved cortex rostral to DM, but
labeled connections were similar to those from injections
that were completely within DM, so any transport from
outside DM was minimal.
Much of the subcortical label was in the pulvinar
complex, which is not highly differentiated in galagos, but
the three major divisions that are typical of primates can
be distinguished (Fig. 2). The inferior pulvinar (PI), which
is a complex of at least four nuclei in monkeys (Stepniewska
and Kaas, 1997), is distinguished by its location between
the LGN and the brachium of the superior colliculus. Clear
subdivisions of the inferior pulvinar in galagos were not
apparent in our AChE, CB, and CO preparations, even
though these preparations allowed four nuclei of the
inferior pulvinar to be delimited in monkeys (see below).
The lateral (PL) and medial (PM) divisions of the pulvinar
were not well differentiated, and they have been commonly
grouped together as the superior pulvinar in previous
studies (Symonds and Kaas, 1978; Raczkowski and Dia￾mond, 1981) Yet, a border between the two divisions can be
approximated in sections processed for CB, because PM is
somewhat darker (Fig. 2B). In AChE- and CO-stained
sections, PL can be somewhat darker. In the LGN, as
others have reported (Casagrande, 1994), the koniocellu￾lar layers are dark in CB preparation and light in CO and
AChE preparations.
Most of the subcortically labeled neurons were in the
lateral and inferior divisions of the pulvinar complex (Figs.
3, 4). At least caudally, the territory of the medial pulvinar
did not contain labeled neurons, and it is possible that the
medial extent of labeled neurons represented the extent of
the lateral pulvinar, with few or no labeled neurons in the
medial pulvinar. In both the lateral and inferior pulvinar,
foci of labeled neurons were scattered and highly overlap￾ping for injections of different tracers placed within 2 mm
of one another, suggesting an overlap of cortical arbors
from pulvinar neurons of 1–2 mm. Yet, there was a
tendency for more rostral injections into DM to label more
dorsal parts of the lateral pulvinar (see, e.g., Fig. 4, section
136). In addition, the clustering of labeled neurons medi￾ally and laterally in the lateral pulvinar, especially ros￾trally in case 95–09 (Fig. 4), suggests the possibility of

Owl monkeys
Descriptions of subcortical connections in owl monkeys
are based on a total of seven injections placed in DM of five
animals, three of which were available from a previous
study on cortical connections (Krubitzer and Kaas, 1993).
Results for two of these cases (94–3 and 93–36) are
illustrated here (Figs. 6, 7). In one animal, a small DY
injection was placed in caudal-lateral DM, and three
larger injections of FR, FB, and WGA-HRP were made into
rostral-medial DM (Fig. 6). In a second animal, a large
WGA-HRP injection was placed in central-rostral DM and
also involved cortex rostral to DM in the ventral posterior
parietal area (Fig. 7). One large injection of WGA-HRP,
placed centrally in dorsal DM, was placed in each of three
additional owl monkeys.
Subdivisions of the pulvinar in owl monkeys could be
distinguished on the basis of staining for CB, CO, and
AChE. The lateral pulvinar could be distinguished as a
large nucleus above the brachium of the superior colliculus
that stained somewhat darker than the medial division in
CB preparations. Subdivisions of the inferior pulvinar
were highly differentiated and were most obvious in
sections stained for CB (for owl monkeys, see Stepniewska
and Kaas, 1997; for squirrel and macaque monkeys, see
Fig. 8). A moderately stained PICL was located most
laterally, and, more medially, PICM stained darkly for CB,
which contrasted with the light staining in PIM. Posterior￾medial PIP showed moderate-to-dense staining for CB.
Divisions were also apparent in CO and AChE prepara￾tions (see Stepniewska and Kaas, 1997). The inferior
pulvinar sometimes extended slightly dorsal to the bra￾chium of the superior colliculus, as reported by others
(Stepniewska and Kaas, 1997).
Most of the labeled neurons were in the lateral and
inferior divisions of the pulvinar complex. In PL, labeled
neurons extended to the border with PM, but no labeled
neurons were found in PM. In the inferior pulvinar, the

PICM (Fig. 7). Overall, labeled neurons were scattered over
a wide extent of PL, PICL, and PICM, and labeled neurons

of extrastriate visual cortex that we define by location and
myeloarchitecture as DM has similar subcortical connec￾tions in prosimian galagos, New World owl and squirrel

claustrum.
In monkeys, in which the application of several histo￾chemical procedures helped to reveal nuclei of the inferior
pulvinar, further similarities in connections were ob￾served. In owl monkeys, the inferior pulvinar was de￾scribed initially with posterior (IPp), medial (IPm), and

Our observations on pulvinar connections are consistent
with the hypothesis that a homologous visual area exists
along the dorsomedial rostral border of V2 in all of these
primates. There is additional evidence from microelec￾trode mapping studies (Krubitzer and Kaas, 1993; Rosa
and Schmid, 1995; Rosa et al., 1997) and studies of

Stepniewska and Kaas, 1997) that the moderately myelin￾ated wedge of cortex we define as DM contains a system￾atic representation of both upper and lower quadrants of
the contralateral field and has inputs from both V1 and V2
in prosimians, New World monkeys, and Old World mon￾keys, as described originally for DM of owl monkeys
(Allman and Kaas, 1975; Wagor et al., 1975; Lin and Kaas,
1979, 1980). Together, these results argue for a consistent
organization of extrastriate visual cortex in the region of
DM across primate taxa. More importantly, the findings
are more compatible with the concept of a single, complete
visual area, DM, than the proposed alternative that the
region consists of a dorsal V3 plus part or all of an
adjoining V3A (for reviews of current theories, see Kaas
1996, 1997).
Although the results are compatible with the proposal
that DM exists in a range of primate species, we do not
know how distinct the pattern of pulvinar connections of
the DM region is from those of other visual areas. Because
adjoining visual areas tend to have similar cortical connec￾tions (Young et al., 1995), similar pulvinar connections are
also expected for adjoining and nearby visual areas. Both
the inferior and lateral pulvinar have been shown to be
connected with a number of visual areas in owl monkeys
(Graham et al., 1979), squirrel monkeys (Cusick et al.,
1993), marmosets (Dick et al., 1991), and macaque mon￾keys (Boussaoud et al., 1992). However, clear differences in
pulvinar connections have been reported for different
cortical areas. For example, we have long known that PIm
is densely interconnected with MT, whereas PIc (PICM and
PICL in the present study) is densely interconnected with
cortex caudal to MT (Lin and Kaas, 1979, 1980). Given the
existence of newly discovered subdivisions of the inferior
pulvinar complex, further study could reveal further differ￾ences in connection patterns. However, we expect that
adjacent areas in the same processing stream (Ungerleider
and Mishkin, 1982) will have similar pulvinar connections
and that areas at different levels of processing and in
different streams will differ more in thalamic connections.
DM seems to have mixed dorsal and ventral stream
cortical inputs, but its major outputs to posterior parietal
cortex identify DM more strongly with the dorsal stream.
However, DM has quite different inferior pulvinar connec￾tions than MT. Whereas MT has dense IPm inputs (Lin
and Kaas, 1980; Wall et al., 1982; Maunsell and Van Essen,
1983; Standage and Benevento, 1983; Ungerleider et al.,
1984; Dick et al., 1991; Cusick et al., 1993), DM injections
labeled very few neurons in PIM. Instead, both V2 and
caudal DL (V4) appear to have dense connections with
PICM and PICL in common with DM (Lin and Kaas, 1979;
Cusick et al., 1993; Stepniewska and Kaas, 1995).

clear evidence that small numbers of neurons in the LGN
of all studied primates project to DM. The LGN of cats has
