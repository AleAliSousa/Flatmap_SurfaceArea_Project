Brain Sizes, Surfaces, and Neuronal Sizes of the Cortex Cerebri: 
A Stereological Investigation of Man and His Variability and a 
Comparison With Some Mammals (Primates, Whales, Marsupials, 
Insectivores, and One Elephant) 
HERBERT HAUG 
Institute for Anatomy, Medical University Lubeck, Federal Republic of Germany 
ABSTRACT This study deals with the stereologi￾cal estimation of macroscopic sizes of brain and cortex, 
i.e., volume, surface, and folding, and of microscopic 
neuronal sizes, i.e., density, mean size, size distribu￾tion, and number of neurons. The results show that the 
degree of variability in man amounts to about 15%. A 
decrease in volume of the different gray structures can 
be observed in man after the age of 65 years. The sur￾face, folding index, and length of convolution do not 
alter with aging. The comparison with mammals of 
various sizes allows the conclusion that there is a high 
correlation to brain size for nearly all macroscopic val￾ues. Man and elephant, however, have a cortical sur￾face which is, in comparison with whales, relatively 
small. In contrast, whales have very small cortices 
compared with man. 
At the cytoarchitectonic level, the neuronal density 
has a correlation to brain size. Contrary to other mam￾mals, the primates and man have a high fraction of 
small granular neurons, especially in layer 4. The as￾sumption that the number of cortical neurons beneath 
a given surface area of cortex is the same in all mam￾mals cannot be verified, especially in those with large 
brains. The allometric connection between brain size 
and parameters is not valid for all measurements (e.g., 
thickness of cortex, mean size of neurons, perikaryal 
size distribution, and glial density). Yet some other 
measurements are well correlated. 
INTRODUCTION 
For about the last 30 years, stereological procedures 
have been increasingly used for investigations of the 
nervous system. Stereology enables us to estimate size 
in all dimensions from macroscopy to light to electron 
microscopy. As to the macroscopic level, Elias et al. 
(1967b) introduced the measurement of the surface area 
of the convoluted cerebral cortex. The estimation of vol￾ume by point counting has been performed since 1956 
by Haug (1956) and Hennig (1958). In the beginning, 
Haug used this procedure for the perikaryon volume￾fraction or gray-cell coefficient of the cortex cerebri 
(Economo, 1926). 
Since 1970, point counting has also been performed for 
the volume estimation of the different gray structures 
in the total brain when working macroscopically (Haug, 
1970a,b; Decker, 1979). In order to measure the mean 
neuronal density in certain parts of the brain, the count￾ing of nerve cells was also stereologically facilitated by 
Haug (1967b). Further important stereological estima￾tions of the brain were reported upon by Stephan (1960), 
Schlenska (1969), and Kuhl et al. (1982). 
The results of these and other similar investigations 
were published in diverse places, and it has been neces￾sary to combine the main efforts of these investigations. 
The size of brain and its parts were mainly published 
regarding man (Elias et al., 1967a,b; Haug, 1970a; 
Schlenska, 1969; Eggers et al., 1984a) and some mam￾mals (Elias et al., 1967a, 1969; Elias and Schwartz, 1969, 
1971; Haug, 1970a, Wiedemeyer, 1974), but some of the 
results of Elias have not been published. At the cytoar￾chitectonic level we find morphometric results for man 
(Haug,1953; Haug et al., 1984; Barmwater, 1982; Kuhl, 
1981; Nass, 1985; Sass,1982a), for large mammals (Haug, 
1967a; Jacobs et al., 1984; Gunschmann, 1972; Tjan, 
1974), for small mammals (Moral and Haug, 1982; Ste￾phan and Andy, 1969; Stephan et al., 1984; Wiedemeyer, 
1974), and for the comparison of mammals (Haug, 1956, 
1958,1960,1976,1981b; Stephan, 1972). 
The findings of our investigation must be combined in 
order to interpret general aspects. The ensuing results 
should be compared with other findings and with var￾ious assumptions about the connections between the 
structural sizes. The mathematical theory, according to 
which different sizes of brain and body are connected, in 
animals, was finally formulated by Dubois (1914). The 
sizes are connected over many decades of exponential 
powers by the formula of “allometry” according to the 
exponential regression y = b.xa. Many papers have 
been presented on this subject. Jerison (1974) has given 
a survey on this theory. 
A second kind of comparison, concerning, in particu￾lar, the information on the extent of evolutionary devel￾opment, was the introduction of indices in which 
different sizes were connected by ratios. The goal of such 
connections is to find an index which expresses a pro￾gressive evolution of a certain neuronal system. The 
first steps were made by Wirz (1950). In recent decades

mean of the species but may lie within a certain, yet

The Macroscopic Sizes of the Brain-Variance and 
Comparison 
In this section the volume of the total brain (Br-vo) 
and of some parts are presented as well as the size and 
configuration of the surface of cortex (Cc-sa; surface area 
in cm3), the folding index (Cc-fi), and the length of con￾volution (Cc-le) of the cortex. Both the latter expressions 
were introduced by Elias et al. (196713). The Cc-fi is an 
expression for the folding of the brain and means the 
ratio between the total surface including all deep parts 
of the convolution and the external surface of the cere￾bral cortex. Cc-le represents the length of those convo￾lutions which can be seen on the exterior surface of the 
cortex. 
The area of the surface of the cortex was estimated for 
the first time by Wagner (1864). His results are very 
similar to the new measurements. The complicated es￾timations were rarely used. The introduction of the new 
stereological procedures, however, opens a new epoch of 
measurement of surface structures. 
The variability of human brain size has been described 
by Haug (1984) in detail. The brain size is influenced by 
the increasing body size resulting from the secular ac￾celeration. Since about the end of the last century, this 
increase in size has been about 1 mm per year. A secular 
acceleration was not observed before the middle of the 
18th century. The size of body had been nearly constant 
for about 2,000 years. The present secular acceleration 
of the body size of 1 mm per year also has led to an 
increase of the brain size of about 0.6 cm3 per year, or to 
50-60 gm per century (Haug, 1984).

The Size Distribution of Cortical Neurons 
The size distributions of the cortical neurons (Ne-sd) 
were primarily estimated from a layerlike measurement 
of flat strips lying parallel to the surface of the cortex. 
In the study presented, however, such a detailed report 
on the results cannot be given. Therefore, our results 
deal with Ne-de in the total depth of the cortex. The 
curves of Figure 11 demonstrate that important differ￾ences exist. The graphs are logarithmic in direction to 
the size and represent the projection area of the neurons. 
This logarithmic scale shows that the larger sizes of 
neurons are apparently represented much less than the 
smaller ones. The logarithmic scale leads to an increas￾ing width of the size classes, contrary to the visual 
impression that the class width becomes smaller. The 
linear heights represent the percentage within the 
classes; this value is corrected depending on the width 
of the classes. 
The density of the neuroiw (1,000 per mm3) is shown 
on the various parts of Figure 11. This density is highly 
different for the various species and areas. It should be 
mentioned, however, that the curves of Ne-sd are based 
on the percentage and not on the density. Another point 
is important, i.e., that small Ne-sd have few classes with 
a high number of neurons. This refers mainly to Ne-sd 
consisting of small cells. On the other hand, the Ne-sd 
with large cells spread their values over many classes. 
Therefore, one can find only a low percentage in each 
single class. 
The arrows in Figure 11 illustrate the mean size of the 
neurons (Ne-ms) in the cortex. If the Ne-sd is almost

DISCUSSION 
As to the general meaning of our results, attention 
should be paid to the fact that the values obtained are 
sampled from mammals of very different families. The 
different procedures used in various measurements by 
other authors must be considered and a basis found for 
the interpretation of data. I shall divide the account into 
three parts. First are the special behavioral patterns 
within one species regarding variation and biological 
changes in aging. In this way we receive information 
about the various measurements and their changes 
which may influence the results obtained from one in￾dividual of a single species. It must be stated that our 
results are corrected for embedding shrinkage and its 
alteration during aging; therefore, they represent val￾ues for fresh material. 
In the second part, the common rules within the mam￾mals studied will be discussed. This part must be con￾nected with the usual assumption of the size of structure 
parameters by the so-called allometric method (Jerison, 
1974). Combined with this view is the third part, which 
deals with the special developments in primates and 
man. In this connection, the comparison of those ani￾mals with large brains and high animal intelligence is 
of great interest. 
Our investigation shows that the variation in all size 
measurements is great. The variance of the interindivi￾dual macroscopic values expressed in percentage of stan￾dard deviation in man is 10-15%. Within this range lie 
about two-thirds of all values of one species. The size 
variation in the cellular structures is between 15 and 
25%. The methodical error is usually smaller than 5% 
during our estimation concerning larger structures or 
structural components. 
If we transfer this variation to the single species of 
mammals in our investigation we are able to say that 
differences in the body and brain size between the indi￾viduals of one species and the average of the species are 
certainly larger than 20%. The differences of the mea￾surements between the various species of mammals are 
larger than 1,000 times. For example, Sorex minutus 
has a brain weight of 0.25 gm and Physeter catodon, 
9,000 gm (Haug, 1970a). Therefore the comparison of 
our results is less influenced by the individual variation 
and the methodical problems. Thus it is reasonable to 
draw comparative conclusions in quantitative terms. 
A further point is important to mention: the age-de￾pendence changes in humans are very complex. The size 
of the brain diminishes after the age of 60-65 years 
(Eggers et al., 1984a, Haug, 1984); however, a large 
individual variance can be observed with respect to the 
age of onset. During biological (physiological) aging, we 
cannot observe a diminution in the number of neurons 
in the human cerebral cortex. The shrinkage of the 
brain during aging, however, leads to an increasing 
density of neurons. In other words, the neurons move 
closer together during aging. This observation transfers 
the changes from the rough cellular to the subcellular 
level during aging (Haug, 1984,1985; Haug et al., 1984). 
A further point must be noted. The age changes in the 
human brain are very different. Those parts of the cere￾bral cortex which are closely connected with the sensory 
functions show fewer signs of aging (area 7 and 17), 
while the motor function areas are strongly affected. 
This can be seen in the extrapyramidal area 6 in which 
the perikarya shrinks to about two-thirds (Eggers et al., 
1984a). An interesting observation in humans is the fact 
that females and males have the same total number of 
neurons, although the size of the female brain is about 
100-150 cm3 smaller than that of the male. The neu￾ronal densities in all five areas investigated were statis￾tically significantly higher in females (Haug, 1985). It is 
unknown whether similar differences between the sexes 
can be found in all higher mammals. This question is 
discussed by Swaab and Hofman (1984) and Dohler and 
Gorski (1981). 
The comparative results at the macroscopic level show, 
for all parameters estimated, a clear relation to brain 
size. This refers to the surface of cortex (Cc-sa) and the 
volume of cortex (Cc-vo), thalamus, and striatum. All 
estimated correlation functions are significant at the 
level of P = .01 or .001.

(19721, Stephan and Andy (19691, and Bauchot (1978) 
have demonstrated a similar behavior. However, the 
examined primates, rodents, and insectivores represent 
only a small sector of the mammals. These scientists did 
not have the opportunity to measure mammals with 
very large brains. Furthermore, they compare the ani￾mals mainly by using brain and body sizes and selected 
indicators such as “progression indices” for brain and 
different brain parts like neocortex. In our results (Figs. 
3a, 4b, 5b), the exponential regression has a slope of

The cellular level, however, gives a quite different 
aspect. The glial cell density (Gl-de) probably does not 
depend on the brain size. A difference between males 
and females does not exist. The G1-de in the gray part of 
the cortex cerebri is at the range of 30,000-60,000 per 
mm3. The G1-de in primates is possibly lower than in 
other mammals, yet we cannot find a statistical 
significance. 
In spite of a high variance, the density of the neurons 
(Ne-de) has a clear connection with brain size, where 
density decreases while brain size increases. However, 
many of the primates and man lie above the regression 
line. In addition to the values presented in Figure 7a, 
Figure 12a contains older estimations from our own and 
other groups. The separately calculated regression func￾tions for primates, including man, and the other mam￾mals show that both groups have a similar slope (-6.0 
and -7.0). The regression for the primates, however, is 
about twice as high as that for the other mammals. 
Similar results were reported upon by Tower (1954) and 
Haug (196713,1981b). 
The mean size of the neuronal perikarya (Ne-ms) has 
no simple relation to brain size. The Old and New World 
monkeys (Figs. 6, 8b, lld) have similar Ne-ms and size 
distributions of neurons (Ne-sd). In the other mammals, 
however, Ne-ms increases with brain size (Bok, 1959). 
Another special evolution in monkeys and man is the 
high fraction of cortical granular neurons with switch￾ing functions. Only in primates does layer lV have a 
typical granular formation consisting mainly of such 
small cells. All other mammals have a layer IV with 
neurons of medium size. Nevertheless, the fibers lying 
parallel to the surface in layer IV represent a common 
development of nearly all mammals.

this is the size of perikaryon-volume fraction (Ne-vo%), 
or the reciprocal gray-cell coefficient which represents 
the size of the neuropil in Cc. The Ne-vo% decreases

The material of our investigation permits an answer 
to the question of whether the number of neurons in a 
certain cylinder of Cc is independent of species and 
family. Bok and Taalman Kip (1939) and Rockel et al. 
(1980) have postulated that this number is, in all mam￾malian Cc, the same below a certain surface area of Cc. 
Only the visual cortex has about double the number in 
such a cylinder. Our square is, with a 1-mm edge size, 
about 1,111 times larger than that calculated by Rockel 
et al. (1980). On the other hand, we also considered the 
three-dimensional shrinkage. 
Our own results concerning Ne-de (1,000 per mm2) can 
almost be compared with those of Bok and Taalman Kip 
by multiplication by a factor of 60 and with Rockel's by 
multiplication by 1,900. Both factors reflect the mean 
shrinkage and the different sizes of surface area of Cc. 
Figure 13 shows our results. The range extends from 
about 19 (1,000 neurons per 100 pm2 times cortex thick￾ness) in the elephant up to 190 in the woolly monkey 
(NL). Also in man we have found large differences in 
neuronal density, between 37 and 92. An unpublished 
personal recalculation of the values of Economo and 
Koskinas (1925) for man has found Ne-de of 35 in area 8 
and over 100 in all primary sensory Cc, with a total 
mean of 50. I think that Rockel's assumption is re￾stricted only to primates, some rodents, and carnivores. 
It cannot be a general rule for all mammals. 
In conclusion, we may state, there is a certain frame￾work of allometric connection within the brain and Cc 
structures within the order of mammals. The allometric 
correlation is relatively high concerning the macro￾scopic measurements. However, the different families 
deviate from this main correlation in a special direction 
for each family (Jerison, 1974). The primates have 
evolved a deviation in the direction of a high fraction of 
granular neurons in Cc. Furthermore, their cortices are 
distinctly thicker compared with other mammals. The 
result is an increasing complexity in the vertical col￾umns of the cortex. The Odontoceti, however, have not 
developed a granular neuronal system. They have in￾creased the Cc folding distinctly more than the pri￾mates; however, we do not know anything about their 
cortical columns. 
The high folding of Cc and the relatively thin Cc in 
whales may be a development which has, on the whole, 
the same functional significance as the development in 
primates. Furthermore, it is possible that the low thick￾ness of Cc in the whales has a greater window function 
of convolution (Prothero and Sundsten, 1984). The win￾dow function is connected with the number of fibers 
which can pass into the space between the gray matter 
of the convolution. This assumption explains the devel￾opment of large brains in whales. Prothero and Sund￾sten (1984) postulated for a 2-mm-thick Cc a brain size 
of about 2,800 cm3 for an optimal function. But accord￾ing to our own observation and those of Kesarev et al. 
(1977) and Morgane and Jacobs (1972), the whales have 
a Cc thickness distinctly less than 2 mm. 
Hans Elias with his remarkable engagement in stereo￾logical procedures in general, and his introduction of 
macroscopic measurements of brain structures as to sur￾face and length of the cerebral convolution, helped to 
obtain a new insight into brain composition. I, person￾ally, have been greatly impressed by his work, and our 
association influenced my macroscopic and microscopic 
survey of the cortex of man with respect to age, varia￾tion, and comparison to other mammals.
