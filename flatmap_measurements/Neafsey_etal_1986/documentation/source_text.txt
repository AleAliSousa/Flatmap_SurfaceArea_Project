Although the location and overall somatotopic or- 
ganization of the rat motor cortex have been known 
for some time”.‘“, the detailed internal organization 
of its larger subdivisions (the forelimb area, for ex- 
ample) has not yet been described. Such a study 
seems warranted because of renewed interest in the 
rat motor cortex prompted by several findings. First, 
Elger et al. l6 have reported that electrical stimulation 
of the rat motor cortex can produce monosynaptic ex-

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Thirty-two adult Long-Evans hooded rats 
(250-500 g) were studied, each of which was anes- 
thetized with ketamine HCI (100 mg/kg i.p., with 
supplemental doses of 114 the initial dose as needed). 
The hair covering the right forelimb and hindlimb 
was clipped to permit better observation of evoked 
movements, and then the animal was placed in a ste- 
reotaxic frame. A heating pad regulated by a temper- 
ature controller maintained the rat’s rectal tempera- 
ture between 36 and 39 “C. After opening the cister- 
na magna to prevent cortical swelling, a craniotomy 
was made over the region of the left frontal or parie- 
tal cortex to be studied. The dura was removed, and 
the exposed cortex was covered with warm saline or 
mineral oil to prevent drying.

3.2.1. Forelimb maps 
Fig. 3 illustrates the results of two typical perpen- 
dicular mapping experiments that attempted to de- 
fine the limits and internal organization of forelimb 
motor cortex. Note first that in each experiment two 
forelimb areas (outlined in heavy lines) were found, a 
large caudal area (CFL) beginning at bregma and a 
small rostra1 area (RFL) located near the frontal 
pole. Cortex where vibrissae (Fig. 3A) or neck (Fig. 
3B) movements were evoked separated the two fore- 
limb areas; the neck movements were observed as ac- 
tual contractions of neck muscle groups seen in the 
surgical exposure used to drain the cistema magna. 
Medial to both forelimb areas, a long parasagittal vi- 
brissae and/or neck movement region was found. It 
was possible in some experiments (Fig. 3A) to find a 
rostra1 hindlimb representation (RHL) just medial to 
the RFL. This RHL was difficult to locate, even 
when it was expressly sought, and threshold currents 
here were usually greater than 40 PA. If a RHL was 
not found, neck (Fig. 3B), trunk or vibrissae move- 
ments were found in its place. Lateral to the RFL. 
jaw and/or tongue movements were evoked; lateral 
to the CFL, vibrissae movements were sometimes 
evoked (Fig. 3A). Histological analysis showed this

chitectonic area of Gr(S1). Caudal to the CFL, in 
many experiments a narrow zone of trunk move- 
ments (Fig. 3A) was found before the hindlimb rep-

were usually evoked laterally while elbow move-

evoked, but a laterally and caudally located shoulder 
movement zone was often found, with elbow and

Note also that the points where a particular move- 
ment was elicited were usually clustered together, 
forming an irregularly shaped patch or zone. This last

3.2.4. Jaw, lips and tongue maps 
Fig. 10 depicts the results of two perpendicular ex- 
periments that were devoted to mapping the jaw, lips 
and tongue area (JLT) of the motor cortex. It is clear 
from the maps that this region was very large relative 
to the other subdivisions of the motor cortex de- 
scribed above. Rostrally and laterally the JLT was 
surrounded by unresponsive points, while medially 
and caudally its border was defined by points where 
vibrissae, neck or forelimb movements were evoked. 
The jaw movement responses usually consisted of 
jaw opening, and such points were most often located 
just lateral to the RFL as is seen in Fig. 10A. Note 
that in this same map there was a second jaw region 
located more laterally, at the rostra1 border of the 
JLT region. In the other map (Fig. 1OB) there was 
only a single jaw point. The lower lip responses con- 
sisted of raising the contralateral lower lip near the 
angle of the jaw. This response was best seen if the 
lower lip was pulled down slightly just prior to stimu- 
lation. This lower lip region varied in size but was al- 
ways found as part of the caudal border of the JLT re- 
gion. Tongue movement responses were of two basic 
types, either strong tongue protrusions (To = tongue 
out) or much weaker twitches of restricted parts of 
the tongue such as the tip (Ttip), the dorsum (Td), 
the ventral surface (TV), or the right (contralateral to 
stimulation) side (Tr = tongue right). In general, the 
strong tongue out responses were located medially 
and rostrally in the JLT region, and the weaker 
tongue twitch responses were found laterally. 
The two experiments with long perpendicular pen- 
etrations parallel to the lateral cortical surface re- 
vealed that the lateral border of the JLT region actu-

animal to animal, but the internal organization of the 
pattern of movements represented within major sub-
