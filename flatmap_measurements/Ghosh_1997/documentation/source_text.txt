Identiﬁcation of Motor Areas of the Cat
Cerebral Cortex Based on Studies of
Cortical Stimulation and Corticospinal
Connections
S. GHOSH*
Department of Physiology and Pharmacology , University of Queensland,
St. Lucia, Brisbane, Queensland 4072, Australia
ABSTRACT
The location and topography of motor areas in the cat cerebral cortex were studied by
electrical stimulation of the cortex in ﬁve animals, and by the injection of retrograde tracers
into the spinal cord of four animals. Movements evoked by intracortical microstimulation
(ICMS) of the anterior, posterior and lateral sigmoid gyri, both banks of the cruciate sulcus
and the dorsal bank of the presylvian sulcus were observed in anaesthetized cats. Fluorescent
tracers (Fast Blue and/or Diamadino Y ellow) were injected into the lateral funiculus in the
second cervical segment, into the gray matter of cervical segments C3–T1 and/or into the gray
matter of lumbar segments L2–S1.
Contraction of the contralateral forelimb, hindlimb or facial muscles was observed
following electrical stimulation of several cytoarchitectonic areas: 4 g,4 d,6 a a,6 a g, and 3a.
These ﬁndings suggested representations of contralateral forelimb and hindlimb movements
in areas 4 g and 4 d, and of the contralateral forelimb muscles in areas 6a a and 6a g.
Corticospinal neurons were located in all the above cytoarchitectonic areas as well as in areas
3b, 1, 2, 2pri, and 5. Large numbers of neurons were labeled in areas 4 g and 4d, and moderate
labeling was observed in areas 6a g and 6aa. Corticospinal neurons projecting to cervical and
lumbar segments were located in areas 4 g and 4 d, while those projecting only to cervical
segments were detected in areas 6a a and 6ag.
Based on these ﬁndings it is proposed that within the motor cortex of the cat there are
representations of limb movements in several cytoarchitectonic subdivisions. Many of these
representations may be candidate secondary motor areas. J. Comp. Neurol. 380:191–214,

The cortical stimulation studies of Woolsey (1958) were
interpreted to recognize, in a number of different mam-
mals, at least two major motor areas controlling limb
movements: the primary motor area (MI) and the supple-
mentary motor area (SMA or MII). Whether these two
areas are homologous across species, however, continues to
be debated (see Wise, 1996). Primates, particularly ma-
caques, have been extensively studied in this regard, and a
lateral premotor area (PM) and a cingulate motor area
(CMA) have also been identiﬁed in these animals (re-
viewed by Wise et al., 1991; Dum and Strick, 1991; Kurata,
1994a). A variety of anatomical and physiological tech-
niques, including cortical stimulation studies, and studies
of corticospinal and corticocortical connections have been
used to identify motor areas of the cortex (reviewed by
Humphrey , 1986; Wise et al., 1991; Dum and Strick, 1991;
Porter and Lemon, 1993; Wise, 1996).
Carnivores, particularly cats, are widely used for the
experimental study of cortical function, including motor
function. Although there have been many studies that
have examined the cytoarchitecture (Hassler and Muhs-
Clements, 1964), motor responses to stimulation (Delgado,

connections (Armand et al., 1974; Groos et al., 1978;
Reinoso-Suarez, 1984; Yumiya and Ghez, 1984) of the cat
motor cortex, many questions remain to be answered.
There appears to be enough data in the literature to locate
the primary motor area in area 4 g (see Avendano et al.,
1992), but the ‘‘secondary’’ motor areas (e.g., SMA, PM)
have not been well characterized in the cat. Data regard-
ing the location of secondary motor areas in other carni-
vores (e.g., dog, raccoon) are also meager (Jameson et al.,
1968; Gorska, 1974).
Many difficulties in the attempts to characterize ‘‘second-
ary’’ motor areas in the cat may be traced to differences in
organization between cat and primate motor areas. For
example, a more or less complete representation of contra-
lateral body parts has been demonstrated in area 4 g but
not in any other parts of area 4 or 6 in the cat (Delgado,
1952; Delgado and Livingston, 1955; Nieoullon and Rispal-
Padel, 1976; Keller, 1993). Corticospinal neurons are sparse
or absent in cytoarchitectonic area 6 (Armand et al., 1974;
Groos et al., 1978) unlike that demonstrated in the pri-
mate (Toyoshima and Sakai, 1982; He et al., 1993, 1995;
Galea and Darian-Smith, 1994).
Since area 4 of the cat cortex is divisible cytoarchitectoni-
cally into several subregions (Hassler and Muhs-Clem-
ents, 1964), it is possible that parts of area 4, particularly
area 4d, may qualify as candidate ‘‘secondary’’ motor areas.
Other studies indicate that area 6 may have projections to
the spinal cord (Hayes and Rustioni, 1981; Catsman-
Berrevoets and Kuypers, 1981; Nudo and Masterton,
1990) and electrical stimulation of area 6 may evoke
movements (Delgado, 1952; Delgado and Livingston, 1955;
Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel, 1976). In order to deﬁne
secondary motor areas in the cat, we have reexamined
area 4 and area 6 of the cat cortex by intracortical
microstimulation studies, and tracer studies of corticospi-
nal connections. The results suggest that parts of area 6
and 4 d project to the spinal cord, have representations of
contralateral body parts, and may qualify as secondary
motor areas.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Experiments were conducted on nine adult cats of either
sex weighing between 2.2 kg and 4 kg. Five animals were
used for cortical stimulation studies while retrogradetracers
were injected into the spinal cord of the other four animals.
All experimental protocols were approved by the University of
Queensland Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee.
Cortical stimulation
Animals were anesthetized with ketamine (5 mg/kg i.m.)
and xylazine (0.05 mg/kg i.m.). Subsequently , light anesthe-
sia was maintained with i.v . Ketamine (3 mg/kg) and pentobar-
bitone (2 mg/kg) based on the appearance of the withdrawal
reﬂex. Atropine (0.06 mg/kg, s.c) and dexamethazone (0.5
mg/kg, i.m.) was administered at the beginning of the
experiment. Blood pressure, expired CO
2 concentration
and rectal temperature were monitored throughout the
experiment. A feedback controlled electric blanket was
used to keep the animal warm (37.5 6 1°C) and intrave-
nous ﬂuids (isotonic saline) administered periodically .
The head was ﬁtted in a sterotaxic frame and the thorax
and abdomen supported with foam cushions in order to
allow the limbs to hang loosely by the side. A craniotomy
was performed on the left side in order to expose the
frontal lobe. The dura was reﬂected over the pericruciate
region and the cortical surface kept moist with warm normal
saline. Glass-coated tungsten electrodes (Re5 0.5MV) were
inserted into the cortex and a train of cathodal pulses (16
pulses, each 0.2 msec wide, frequency 400 Hz) was deliv-
ered at depths from 1.5 mm to 11.0 mm at 1 or 1.5 mm
intervals. The stimulus current was monitored and varied
from 1 to 100 µA. The cortex was sampled in a grid-like
manner at intervals of 1 mm (avoiding surface vessels) and
penetrations were extended posteriorly up to the caudal
end of the ansate sulcus in order to reach the depths of the
cruciate sulcus. Lesions were frequently made (by passing
10 µA for 10 seconds) when movements were evoked from
cortical regions deeper than 3–4 mm. At least one lesion
was made in most successful tracts. However, sometimes
we avoided making too many closely spaced lesions by not
lesioning in adjacent penetrations at similar depths.
At least two observers were present throughout each
experiment in order to identify the movements evoked by
intracortical microstimulation (ICMS). Observation and
palpation of muscle contraction was used to identify
evoked movements. Limbs were repositioned if initial
trials were unsuccessful. Current thresholds for evoking
movements were also determined by both observers.

The ﬂuorescent tracers, Fast Blue (FB, Sigma) and
Diamadino Y ellow (DY , Sigma) were injected into the
spinal cord of four animals (cases 6 to 9). Anesthesia was
induced by ketamine and xylazine and maintained with
ketamine and pentobarbitone administered intramuscu-
larly (see above for dosages). Atropine and steroid were
administered preoperatively (see above). The rectal tem-
perature was monitored and the animal kept warm using a

Perfusion and histological procedures
At the end of the ICMS experiments or after appropriate
survival times (2–4 weeks after tracer injection in the
spinal cord) the animal was deeply anaesthetized (i.v .
pentobarbitone 50 mg/kg) and perfused with 2 liters of
normal saline followed by 2 liters of 4% paraformaldehyde
in phosphate buffer. The brain was removed, photo-
graphed and soaked in 30% sucrose. The spinal cord was
removed from animals with tracer injections.
Each hemisphere was cut serially on a freezing micro-
tome (50 µm). Brain sections from cats subjected to cortical
stimulation were (every alternate 50 µm section) collected
and mounted onto slides. The sections were stained with
cresyl violet or thionine, dehydrated, cleared in xylene and
coverslipped. The sections were then examined for elec-
trode tracts and lesions (Fig. 2).
Brain and spinal cord (appropriate segments) sections
from cats with tracer injections were divided into four
batches. The ﬁrst batch was mounted on slides, dehy-
drated, cleared and coverslipped for examination of ﬂuores-
cent label. The second batch was stained with cresyl violet
or thionine for cytoarchitectonic study .
Identiﬁcation of injection sites and labeled
cortical neurons
Cresyl violet-stained sections were drawn at a magniﬁ-
cation of 44 through a binocular microscope (Zeiss Ax-
ioskop) with a drawing tube (objective 310, drawing tube
34.4, Fig. 3). The unstained section was then projected to
overlap the drawing accurately , and labeled neuronal
proﬁles were identiﬁed and plotted on the drawing. The
labeled proﬁles were then observed under higher power
(objective 320, 350) to conﬁrm plotted points or identify
double labeled neurons (Fig. 3). The injection sites in the
spinal cord were similarly documented. Cytoarchitectonic
borders of the cerebral cortex were included in the draw-
ing.
Identiﬁcation of cytoarchitectonic
boundaries
Cytoarchitecture of the motor and somatosensory areas
of the cerebral cortex was based on Hassler and Muhs-
Clements (1964; Figs. 4–7). Subsequently , other studies
(Avendano and Verdu, 1992; Avendano et al., 1992; LeClerc
et al., 1994; Myasnikov et al., 1994) have elaborated and
modiﬁed some of these criteria. One of the major changes
proposed by Avendano et al. (1992) was the subdivision of
area 6ab into a laterally located 6ag and a medially located
6ab. Further, the boundary of area 6a a was extended
rostrally , so that it occupied a portion of the cortex in the
anterior sygmoid gyrus. We followed the newer descrip-
tions of Avendano et al. (1992) because they also distin-
guished parts of area 6 with and without corticospinal
projections.
Hassler and Muhs-Clements (1964) subdivided area 4
into four cytoarchtectonic areas: 4 g,4 d, 4 suprafundum
(4sfu) and 4 in fundo (4fu). Of these, only 4 g contains large
numbers of giant pyramidal cells, often arranged in mul-
tiple rows. The width of area 4 d cortex is smaller and giant
pyramidal cells are less frequent (Fig. 4). The cortical
width in areas 4sfu and 4fu decreases further and the
pyramidal cells in layer V are even smaller. The overall
thickness of the cortex diminishes gradually with increas-
ing depth in the cruciate sulcus and at the border of areas
4g and 4d the thickness of these two areas may be similar
(Fig. 2) although the size and clustering of pyramidal cells
in lamina V still allow distinction of the two areas.
Differences in the width and cellular arrangement in
lamina III also distinguish the different subdivisions of
area 4 (Hassler and Muhs-Clements, 1964). The exact
borders between areas 4d, 4fu and 4sfu are not always easy
to deﬁne but the illustrated margins are within 50–200 µm
of clearly distinguishable cytoarchitecture.
Hassler and Muhs-Clements (1964) distinguished three
subdivisions within area 6: area 6a a,6 a b and 6iffu. Area
6ab (as deﬁned by Hassler and Muhs-Clements, 1964)
involved a wide region of cortex including the medial
surface of the cortex (below the cruciate sulcus), a part of
the rostral or ventral bank of the cruciate sulcus, the
anterior sygmoid gyrus and the dorsal bank of the presyl-
vian sulcus. Subsequently , Avendano et al. (1992) have
pointed out cytoarchitectonic variations within area 6a b,
which were also conﬁrmed in our study . We found that area
6ab is conﬁned to a thin strip of cortex in the medial part of
the anterior sigmoid gyrus, and a thin strip of cortex
adjoining the cruciate sulcus on the medial surface and in
the rostral (ventral) bank of the cruciate sulcus. Layer V in

Area 3a is distinguishable from area 4 g by the presence
of a thin lamina IV , and an overall reduction in cortical
thickness, but the latter property is not always evident
(Fig. 7). Large pyramidal cells are frequently located in
lamina V of area 3a (Avendano and Verdu, 1992). Area 3b
has a thick and prominent lamina IV (Fig. 7). In lateral
cortical regions, lamina V is light with no large pyramidal
cells (Fig. 7), but more medially , large pyramidal cells may
be frequently seen (LeClerc et al., 1994).
RESULTS
Electrical stimulation of the motor cortex
Intracortical microstimuli were delivered to the ante-
rior, posterior and lateral sigmoid gyri. In addition, the
banks of the cruciate sulcus and the dorsal bank of the
presylvian sulcus were explored. The dorsal surface of the
cortex was explored in a grid-like manner at intervals of 1
mm between adjacent penetrations, avoiding surface ves-
sels. Electrode tracts and lesions were reconstructed from
histological sections in order to locate the area stimulated
(Fig. 8). The stimulated points and the joints moved were
then mapped on the cortical surface and in the banks of the
cruciate and presylvian sulci (Figs. 9–11). The electrode
tracts were sometimes found to deviate from the vertical in
the depths of the cortex (Fig. 8). Therefore, the exploration
of the depths of the cruciate and presylvian sulci was not
uniform (Figs. 9, 10).
The cortical surface was reconstructed from coronal
sections (in the plane of the upper border of lamina V or
lower border of lamina IV) as follows. The medial edge of
the hemisphere was used as the reference to align all
sections and the distances between this edge and various
sulci and the inferior edge of the hemisphere were mea-
sured and plotted. The points were then joined using a
smooth line. The banks of the presylvian sulcus and
cruciate sulcus were unfolded separately at the upper
border of lamina V . The cytoarchitectonic boundaries were
then included in the drawing.
The ICMS maps from cases 1 and 2 are presented in
Figures 9 and 10, respectively , while Figure 11 represents
results from all ﬁve animals studied. ICMS was effective in
evoking movements from areas 4 g,4 d,6 aa,6 ag, and area
3a. Although stimuli in areas 1 and 2 were also effective,
these areas were not studied in detail; hence, data are not
included. Electrical stimuli were ineffective in areas 6a b,
6iffu and 4sfu. Within the areas from which movements
were successfully evoked, many stimulated points were
ineffective. In such situations, trials were repeated with
the limbs repositioned and after a short delay (5–10
minutes), success was obtained at some points.
Stimulation of the banks of the cruciate sulcus or
presylvian sulcus was usually only effective when the
stimulated point was in lamina V or VI (Fig. 8). Stimuli
delivered at more superﬁcial cortical laminae rarely evoked
movements, required high currents, and were not included
in illustrations or in analysis of current thresholds. Stimu-
lus points located in white matter were not analyzed.
Movements were frequently evoked contralaterally in
the forelimb or hindlimb. These were invariably ﬂexion
movements, though occasionally pronation and supination
movements appeared to be superimposed on wrist ﬂexion.
Similarly , abduction and adduction, in addition to ﬂexion,
was sometimes observed at the shoulder or hip joints.
Facial movements involved the contralateral eyelids,
cheeks or lips; jaw movements were not observed. Since
facial movements were infrequently evoked in our study ,
the location of the cortical representation of facial muscle
must be cautiously interpreted. Forelimb movements were
evoked from areas 4 g,4 d,6 a a and 6a g, hindlimb move-
ments from areas 4 g and 4 d and facial movements from
areas 4g,6 aa and 6ag.
In dealing with somatotopic organization within these
areas, we decided to subdivide areas 4 g and 4d into rostral
(r4g,r 4 g) and caudal (c4 g,c 4 d) sectors, separated by the
cruciate sulcus (or its fundus) for several reasons. First, it
has been suggested that electrical thresholds for evoking
movements are lower in r4 g than c4 g (Nieoullon and
Rispal-Padel, 1976). Secondly , separate representations of
the forelimb and hindlimb in r4 g and c4g are suggested by
the study of Groos et al. (1978). Thirdly it has been shown

Tracer injections in the spinal cord
Fluorescent tracer injections result in three concentric
zones of labeling: an inner zone with necrotic material and
bright tissue ﬂuorescence; a middle zone of bright tissue
ﬂuorescence and labeled neurons and glia; and an outer
zone with no tissue ﬂuorescence and less intensely labeled
neurons and glia (Fig. 1, Keizer et al., 1983). The two inner
zones were taken as constituting the zone of uptake. The
single tracer injection in case 6 involved a large part of the
lateral funiculus and included most if not all of the lateral
corticospinal tract (Fig. 1; Nyberg-Hansen and Brodal,
1963; Armand et al., 1985). The tracer injections in cases
7–9 involved part of the dorsal columns, a large part of the
intermediate zone of the gray matter, and a variable part
of the dorsal and ventral horn (Figs. 1 and 13). The tracer
injection never spread into the lateral funiculus but occa-
sionally came close to the anterior funiculus (Figs. 1 and
13). However, we felt that involvement of the ventral
corticospinal tract was unlikely . Each injection involved
about 2–3 mm of the spinal cord rostrocaudally . However,
there were segments of the cord between penetrations that
were not involved by the tracer. Close to the injection site,
labeled neurons were observed profusely throughout the
ipsilateral gray matter and less profusely in the contralat-
eral intermediate zone and ventral horn. Motoneurons
were well labeled bilaterally .
Corticospinal neurons
Neurons labeled following tracer injections in the lateral
funiculus or gray matter of the spinal cord are mapped in
Figures 14–16. The mapping procedure is similar to that
adopted for presentation of ICMS data. All corticospinal
neurons were located in lamina V of the cortex and labeled
in clusters (Groos et al., 1976; Jones and Wise, 1977;
Murray and Coulter, 1981; Hayes and Rustioni, 1981;
Nudo and Masterton, 1990). In all the animals studied,
motor areas 4g,4 d,6 aa, and 6ag, and somatosensory areas
3a, 3b, 1, 2, 2pri, and 5 were well labeled. A few cells were
located in the insula. Labeled cells were rarely located in
areas 6a b, 6iffu, 4sfu, or 4fu. In the animal (case 9) with
dual tracer injections in the cervical and lumbar segments
of the cord, double labeled neurons were rarely seen (Fig.
15).
Dense labeling was observed in areas 4 g and in area 4 d
in all animals. Labeling in area 6 was observed only
following tracer injection in the cervical cord, in the gray
matter or in the lateral funiculus. Labeled neurons were
distributed throughout area 4 g, but parts of area 4 d,6 a a
and 6a g were unlabeled in all the four animals (Figs.
14–16). The medial part of area 6a a and r4 d, the deeper
parts of c4 d, and parts of area 6a g were consistently
unlabeled.

Comparison of ICMS and tracer results
The data obtained from the ICMS (Figs. 9–11) and tracer
(Figs. 14–16) studies were complementary . ICMS was
successful only in those motor areas which subsequently
were shown to possess corticospinal neurons. Similarly ,
representations of forelimb and hindlimb movements in
r4g,c 4 g,r 4 d, and c4 d, and the representation of forelimb
movements in areas 6a a and 6a g, are suggested by both
sets of data.
DISCUSSION
Motor areas have been deﬁned operationally as those
regions of the cortex from which movements or brief
muscle contractions are evoked with lowest threshold
and/or shortest latency by applied electrical stimuli (Hum-
phrey , 1986). It has been known for a long time that
mapping of motor cortical areas using electrical stimuli,
using relatively low stimulus currents, is dependant greatly
upon direct corticospinal projections (reviewed by Phillips
and Porter, 1977; Kuypers, 1982; Humphrey , 1986; Porter
and Lemon, 1993). Therefore, it may be expected that a
combination of approaches, utilizing electrical stimulation
of the cortex and neuroanatomical tracing of corticospinal
connections, would provide mutually complementary re-
sults and that such an approach is particularly valuable in
deﬁning motor areas in animals that lack a signiﬁcant
corticomotoneuronal connection (e.g., the cat, Lundberg
and Voorhoeve, 1962). This study has therefore utilized the
combined approach to demonstrate multiple representa-
tions of the contralateral limbs in the motor cortex of the
cat. A similar approach has been used to deﬁne the borders
of the primary motor area in macaques (Sessle and Wiesen-
danger, 1982).

Cortical mapping by electrical stimulation
Electrical stimulation spreads physically and physiologi-
cally (Asanuma and Rosen, 1973; Jankowska et al., 1975;
Asanuma et al., 1976; Phillips and Porter, 1977) and
therefore cannot always resolve the exact limits of the
‘‘excitable’’ cortex, nor clearly deﬁne topographical repre-
sentation of movement where there is considerable overlap
or interdigitation of the representation of different body
parts. The use of large currents for ICMS may provide
misleading results. Nevertheless, electrical stimulation
studies, particularly in the macaque monkey (using a
similar range of currents), have provided information on
cortical topography not only in the MI, but also in the PM,
SMA and the CMA (Asanuma and Rosen, 1972; Anderson
et al., 1975; Kwan et al., 1978; MacPherson et al., 1982;
Mitz and Wise, 1987; Rizzolatti et al., 1988; Luppino et al.,
1991). The presence of corticomotoneuronal connections in
primates, particularly involving distal limb muscles (re-
viewed by Porter and Lemon, 1993), allows the use of
smaller currents for evoking movements and a greater
resolution in cortical cartography . However, even in the
cat, relatively small currents ( ,20 µA) were effective in a
proportion of the points stimulated in each of the ‘‘excit-
able’’ areas.
The results of our microstimulation study agree with the
previous ﬁndings of Keller (1993), Nieoullon and Rispal-
Padel (1976), Delgado (1952) and Delgado and Livimgston
(1955). However, in our study , movements of facial muscles
were evoked infrequently and jaw and back muscles could
never be activated. In addition, retraction of claws was
also not evoked. These differences may in part be due to
the differences in the preparation, including the state of
consciousness and depth of anaesthesia, type of anaes-
thetic used, the techniques utilized to monitor muscle
contraction (e.g., visual observation, e.m.g. recording or
transducer techniques), electrode resistance and stimulus
currents (Delgado, 1952; Schlag and Schlag-Rey , 1970;
Asanuma and Ward, 1971; Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel,
1976; Pappas and Strick, 1981; Iwata et al., 1990; Keller,
1993). Some studies (e.g., Pappas and Strick, 1981; Keller,
1993) that have made a more detailed study of a part of
area 4 g (involving mainly or exclusively the forelimb
representation), have also been able to detect many more
low threshold points for evoking movements of forelimb
and facial muscles.
Our study found representation of limb movements in
several cytoarchitectonic subdivisions of the motor cortex:
areas 4 g,4 d,6 a a, and 6a g. While there was considerable
overlap of stimulus currents required to elicit movements
from these areas, the mean thresholds were lowest in area
4g and higher in areas 4 d,6 aa and 6ag.
Corticospinal projections in the cat
Corticospinal projections were investigated using tracer
injections in the lateral funiculus or intermediate gray
matter of the spinal cord. In both instances, it is unlikely
that all corticospinal axons or their terminals were ac-
cessed. The tracer injection in the lateral funciculus was
located in the region of the lateral corticospinal tract
(Armand et al., 1985). Damage to the lateral corticospinal
tract was evident, so that the tracer (Fast Blue) was taken
up by both damaged and undamaged axons (Payne, 1987).
Fibers descending in the ventral corticospinal tract, how-
ever, were not sampled (Armand and Kuypers, 1980). It
has been estimated that the pyramidal tract of the cat
contains about 400,000 ﬁbers (both myelinated and unmy-
elinated, Thomas et al., 1984) and the total (estimated)
number of neuronal proﬁles labeled (in the contralateral
hemisphere) in our experiments varied from 14,000 to
16,000. Whereas not all ﬁbers of the pyramidal tract
neurons descend into the spinal cord, it would still appear
that less than 10% of the corticospinal neurons were
labeled in each experiment.
Tracer injections were directed to the intermediate zone
of the gray matter in order to involve corticospinal termina-
tions, particularly from the motor areas (Nyberg-Hansen
and Brodal, 1963; Armand et al., 1985). Other studies have
utilized this technique to investigate corticospinal projec-
tions (Groos et al., 1978; Hayes and Rustioni, 1981; He et
al., 1993, 1995). Whereas a large part of the intermediate
zone was involved in the region of tracer injections, the
involvement of the dorsal and ventral horn was usually
small and variable. Therefore, the proportion of corticospi-
nal neurons labeled in the somatosensory areas may be
relatively smaller than in the motor area because the
projections from the former area terminate mainly in the
dorsal horn (Nyberg-Hansen and Brodal, 1963; Armand et
al., 1985).
The use of multiple retrograde tracers to investigate the
topography of descending projections provides many advan-
tages (see Catsman-Berrevoets and Kuypers, 1981; Hayes
and Rustioni, 1981; He et al., 1993, 1995). In this study , the
descending projections to cervical and lumbar segments of
the spinal cord were compared in one animal using two
tracers, and in two other animals, tracers were injected
either in the cervical or lumbar cord. It was important to
ensure that decending tracts were not involved in the
tracer injections at the cervical levels; hence, penetrations
were made through the dorsal columns (Groos et al., 1978;
Hayes and Rustioni, 1981; He et al., 1993, 1995) and the
injections made at depths that precluded involvement of
the ventral funiculus.
Corticospinal projections were found to arise from a
number of agranular regions. Although the bulk of the
cortical labeling was located in area 4 g, a number of
neurons were also labeled in areas 4 d,6 aa and 6ag. These
ﬁndings agree with other reports of the location of cortico-
spinal neurons in area 6 (Catsman-Berrevoets and
Kuypers, 1981; Hayes and Rustioni, 1981; Nudo and
Masterton, 1990). In our study , labeling in area 6a a was
sparse and that in area 6ag was moderate, suggesting that
contribution to the corticospinal tract from area 6 is much
smaller than from area 4.
Corticospinal neurons projecting to both cervical and
lumbar segments were rare. Whereas individual corticospi-
nal axons arborize extensively in the rostrocaudal dimen-
sions of the spinal cord (Shinoda et al., 1976, 1986), few
appear to project to both cervical and lumbar segments
(Shinoda et al., 1976, 1986; Hayes and Rustioni, 1981).
This study provides evidence for at least three represen-
tations of the contralateral limb(s) outside area 4 g. One of
these is located in the depth of the caudal bank of the
cruciate sulcus, involving area c4d and the adjacent part of
c4g. The representation of forelimb and hindlimb move-
ments was also detected in r4d. In these regions, there was
considerable interdigitation of forelimb and hindlimb re-
lated areas (see discussion in Groos et al., 1978). A second
representation of the contralateral forelimb was detected

Motor areas in the cat
Many studies have examined the representation of
movement in the motor cortex of the cat (MacKibben and
Wheelis, 1932; Ward and Clark, 1935; Delgado, 1952;
Delgado and Livingston, 1955; Woolsey , 1964; Asanuma
and Sakata, 1967; Asanuma et al., 1968; Asanuma and
Ward, 1971; Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel, 1976; Pappas
and Strick, 1981; Keller, 1993) and provided a detailed
account of the topography of movement representation in
the primary motor area (Delgado, 1952; Delgado and
Livingston, 1955; Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel, 1976; Keller,
1993). Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel (1976) used ICMS in
cats chronically implanted with electrodes to reveal a
detailed representation of the contralateral head, neck,
face, back, forelimb, and hindlimb muscles. Their studies
suggested a single representation of these structures with
considerable overlap or interdigitation, and involving area
4g, area 6a a, and possibly area 6a g (see Fig. 5 of their
study). In their scheme, hindlimb movements were repre-
sented mainly in the posterior sigmoid gyrus and in the
caudal bank of the cruciate sulcus, forelimb movements in
the lateral sigmoid gyrus and adjoining parts of the
anterior and posterior sigmoid gyri, and face and head
movements in areas 6a a and 6a g. Although a part of the
caudal (or dorsal) bank of the cruciate sulcus was explored,
many other regions in the banks of the cruciate sulcus or
the presylvian sulcus were not examined. Keller’s (1993)
study involved area 4 g, and possibly area 6a g, and sug-
gested that the representation of facial muscles is located
lateral to that of forelimb muscles. Delgado (1952) and
Delgado and Livingston’s (1955) studies found a represen-
tation of cranial and forelimb muscles in the dorsal bank of
the presylvian sulcus. Whereas there are suggestions that
certain movements or muscles may be represented more
than once in the motor cortex (e.g., Pappas and Strick,
1981; Keller, 1993), all previous studies have implied an
overall single representation of contralateral body parts in
the motor cortex of the cat.
Studies of the representation of movement in other
carnivores (racoons and dogs), on the other hand, have
suggested a dual representation of the body in the motor
cortex, separated by the cruciate sulcus: MI being located
caudal to the SMA (Hardin et al., 1968; Jameson et al.,
1968; Gorska, 1974). In these studies, current thresholds
were found to be higher in the SMA, and more complex and
bilateral movements were evoked from this area.
This study found representations of both contralateral
limbs, using both ICMS and tracer studies, in two cytoar-
chitectonic areas of the agranular cortex: area 4 g and area
4d. In addition, the forelimb was found to be represented in
areas 6aa and 6ag. Further, representation of forelimb and
hindlimb muscles was detected in both rostral and caudal
subdivisions of 4g and 4d. Facial muscles were found to be
represented in areas 6a a,6 ag and in a small part of area
4g. Whereas these ﬁndings are in broad agreement with
previous results, they also suggest multiple representa-
tions of contralateral body parts in the motor cortex of
the cat.
For a number of reasons, area 4 g has been considered to
be the primary motor area in the cat. These include
cytoarchitectonic criteria, low current requirements for
evoking movements by cortical stimulation, and the pres-
ence of large numbers of corticospinal neurons. Neverthe-
less, while most studies have found large regions of 4 g
involved in controlling limb movements, many studies
(including ours) found it difficult to localize a face area in
4g. Labeled corticospinal neurons were found to involve
practically the whole extent of area 4 g, leaving free only a
small part adjacent to area 6a a, from which facial move-
ments were occasionally evoked. It appears that a substan-
tial part of the representation of facial movements in cat
motor cortex lies outside area 4 g. Previous studies have
included the representation of facial muscles in either area
6aa,6 ag, or both, within MI (Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel,
1976; Keller, 1993). The present study found that facial
movements could be evoked with relatively small currents
(,50 µA) from a few points within area 6a g, whereas
current requirements appeared to be higher in area 6a a
(always . 50 µA, Fig. 11). In addition, Keller (1993), Iwata
et al. (1990) and Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel’s (1976)
studies also appear to identify area 6a g as the low thresh-
old site for evoking facial movements. Thus, one scheme of
the topographic organization in cat MI could envisage a
mediolaterally oriented representation of hindlimb, fore-
limb and facial movements, respectively , and including
areas 4g and 6ag.
A representation of forelimb muscles was also detected
in the dorsal (or lateral) lip and bank of the presylvian
sulcus. This representation of forelimb muscles in area 6ag
appears to involve both proximal and distal musculature
and is the site of origin of large numbers of corticospinal
neurons. Area 6a g has been considered a part of the
primary MI by previous studies, because a representation
of facial muscles has been difficult to detect in the lateral
parts of area 4 g. Nevertheless, cytoarchitectural and con-
nectional differences (see below), and differences in cur-
rents required for evoking movements suggest an alterna-
tive possibility: that the representation of forelimb
movements in area 6a g may be separate from that in area
4g. The representation of facial muscles in area 6a g is also
not homogeneous. Iwata et al. (1990) distinguish two
separate representations: one in anterior part of the
coronal and lateral sigmoid gyri (C-S motor zone) and
another in the dorsal (lateral) bank of the presylvian
sulcus (P motor zone) on the basis of ICMS effects and
responses to peripheral stimulation. Thus, if the C-S zone
is considered to be part of MI, then a second representation
of forelimb and facial muscles (P-zone) can be envisaged in
the dorsal (lateral) bank of the presylvian sulcus. This
region could possibly be a homologue of the primate PMv
(see below).
A second interpretation of results could visualize the
representation of facial muscles in the medial edge of r4 g
(and possibly including that in area 6a a) as part of MI,
with a complete body map folded across the cruciate sulcus
(Nieoullon and Rispal-Padel, 1976). Further studies may
be required to indicate whether representations of fore-
limb and facial movements in area 6a a and 6ag are part of
or separate from the primary motor cortical representation
of these movements.

Comparison with primates
The structure, function and connections of motor areas
have been studied most rigorously in macaques. Based on
studies of cortical architecture, stimulation, connections,
lesions, and association of neural activity with motor
performance, at least four major motor areas controlling
limb movements have been identiﬁed in this species: MI,
SMA, PM, and CMA (reviewed by Wise et al., 1991; Dum
and Strick, 1991; Kurata, 1994a). These areas can be
further subdivided on the basis of structural and func-
tional differences (e.g., pre-SMA and SMA, PMv and PMd,
CMAr, CMAv and CMAd; Rizzolatti et al., 1988; Luppino
et al., 1991; Kurata, 1991, 1994a; He et al., 1993, 1995;
Ghosh and Gattera, 1995). Many of these areas have also
been identiﬁed in other primates (Gould et al., 1986;
Stepniewska et al., 1993; Wise, 1996). However, inspite of the
availability of detailed information in primates, there are few
properties, if any , that can be used to unequivocally identify a
particular motor area (see Stepniewska et al., 1993; Wise,
1996). It is also clear that boundaries between primary and
nonprimary motor areas can rarely be deﬁned by ICMS or by
the mapping of corticospinal neurons alone (Sessle and Wiesen-
danger, 1982; Mitz and Wise, 1987; Rizzolatti et al., 1988;
Nudo and Masterton, 1990; He et al., 1993, 1995).
Whereas MI is considered to be located in area 4 g of the
cat, there are both similarities and differences between cat
and primate MI. Similarities include cytoarchitecture, the
presence of large numbers of corticospinal neurons and the
requirement of relatively low currents to evoke move-
ments by stimulation. MI of both cats and monkeys receive
input predominantly from ‘‘cerebellar’’ thalamic territories
(Jones et al., 1979; Schell and Strick, 1984; Yumiya and
Ghez, 1984; Ghosh et al., 1987; Moran and Reinoso-
Suarez, 1988; Inase and Tanji, 1995). There are major
differences, between the two orders, in the projection and
termination of corticospinal axons in the spinal cord
(reviewed by Phillips and Porter, 1977). In this and
previous studies, it was noted that the face representation
in area 4 g is much smaller than that in primate MI (see
above). In addition, separate rostral and caudal subdivi-
sions can be identiﬁed in cat MI which do not appear to
correspond to the rostral and caudal subdivisions identi-
ﬁed in primate MI (Stepniewska et al., 1993). Firstly ,
ICMS thresholds appear to be lower in r4g than in c4g, and
secondly , there are few connections, if any , between r4 g
and c4 g in the cat (Yumiya and Ghez, 1984) unlike that
seen in the primate (Stepniewska et al., 1993).
The SMA is located rostromedial to MI in primates, is
somatotopically organized, and projects to the spinal cord
and MI (reviewed by Wise, 1996). Whereas current thresh-
olds for evoking movements are generally held to be higher
in SMA, this has not always been found to be so (e.g., Mitz
and Wise, 1987; see Wise, 1996). In addition, there are
suggestions that thalamic territories receiving inputs from
the basal ganglia project preferentially to SMA, although
‘‘cerebellar’’ thalamic projections to SMA have also been
described (Schell and Strick, 1984; Weisendanger and
Wiesendanger, 1985; Darian-Smith et al., 1990; Nakano et
al., 1993). From the above descriptions, it would be difficult
to consider a homologue of primate SMA in r4 g of the cat,
as has been suggested in other carnivores (see above).
Thresholds in r4 g appear to be lower than c4 g, and
projections from r4g to c4g are sparse or absent in the cat.
Further, although thalamic projections to r4 g and c4g are
clearly different, predominantly ‘‘cerebellar’’ thalamic ter-
ritories project to both areas (Yumiya and Ghez, 1984;
Moran and Reinoso-Suarez, 1988). It would be equally
difficult to locate SMA in area 6a a, because corticospinal
connections from this area are sparse, and projections
from 6aa to area 4 g are few and restricted to r4 g (Yumiya
and Ghez, 1984). An alternative candidate for SMA in the
cat is area 4 d, which has large numbers of corticospinal
neurons and a large representation of the contralateral
limbs. In addition, studies in our laboratory have revealed
somatotopically organized reciprocal connections between
areas 4 g and 4 d (unpublished observations). However,
whereas the origin of thalamic projections to area 4 d is
different from that to area 4 g, predominantly ‘‘cerebellar’’
thalamic territories project to area 4d (Moran and Reinoso-
Suarez, 1988).
