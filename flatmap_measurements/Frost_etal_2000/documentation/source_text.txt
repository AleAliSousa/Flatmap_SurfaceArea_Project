ABSTRACT
To examine the potential early stages in the evolution of sensorimotor cortex, electrophysi-
ological studies were conducted in the primitive South American marsupial opossum,Monodel-
phis domestica.Somatosensory maps derived from multiunit microelectrode recordings revealed
a complete somatosensory representation of the contralateral body surface within a large region
of midrostral cortex (primary somatosensory cortex, or S1). A large proportion (;51%) of S1 was
devoted to representation of the glaborous snout, mystacial vibrissae, lower jaw, and oral cavity
(the rostrum). A second representation, the second somatosensory area (or S2), was found
adjacent and caudolateral to S1 as a mirror image reversed along the representation of the
glabrous snout. A reversal of somatotopic order and an enlargement of receptive ﬁelds marked
the transition from S1 to S2. Mapping of excitable cortex was conducted by using intracortical
microstimulation (ICMS) techniques, as well as low-impedance depth stimulation and bipolar
surface stimulation. In all three procedures, electrical stimulation resulted in movements con-
ﬁned strictly to the face. Speciﬁcally, at virtually all sites from which movements could be evoked,
stimulation resulted in only vibrissae movement. ICMS-evoked vibrissae movements typically
occurred at sites within S1 with receptive ﬁelds of the mystacial vibrissae, lower jaw, and
glaborous snout. Results were similar using low-impedance depth stimulation and bipolar sur-
face stimulation techniques except that the motor response maps were generally larger in area.
There was no evidence of a motor representation rostral to S1. Examination of the cytoarchitec-
ture in this cortical region (reminiscent of typical mammalian somatosensory cortex) and the high

Indexing terms: sensory cortex; motor cortex; marsupial; evolution
For at least 50 years, it has been suggested that the
evolution of the mammalian sensorimotor cortex was
marked by progressive differentiation into separate sen-
sory and motor areas. This notion gained widespread sup-
port after a series of electrophysiological experiments by
Richard Lende in the 1960s using surface stimulation and
evoked potential techniques in the North American opos-
sum (Didelphis virginiana). Based on these experiments,
Lende argued that the sensory and motor areas of neuro-
logically primitive mammals (e.g., opossums) overlap com-
pletely. He referred to this area as a “sensorimotor amal-
gam” (Lende, 1963a,b,c).
Recently, we reexamined the notion of a sensorimotor
amalgam in the gray, short-tailed (or Brazilian) opossum
(Monodelphis domestica). This species was chosen because
in a previous comparative study of 24 mammals, it was
found to possess relatively few corticospinal neurons, pos-
sibly indicating that its cortical motor system is neurolog-
ically primitive (Nudo and Masterton, 1988, 1990a). Of the
species examined in tract tracing studies by Nudo and
Masterton, the Monodelphis opossum was found to have
the second lowest number of corticospinal neurons (second
only to the least shrew), the lowest number of corticospi-
nal neurons per brain weight, and the smallest proportion
of cortex containing corticospinal neurons (Nudo and Mas-
terton, 1990a,b; Nudo et al., 1995).
M. domestica is a small terrestrial marsupial native to
the semiarid region of northeastern Brazil (Strilein, 1982).
Like the North American opossum, Monodelphis is part of
the family of Didelphid opossums which, based on com-
parative neurology of the past century, has retained the
basic brain structure that is believed to closely approxi-
mate the form which can serve as a starting point for
tracing the evolution of modern mammalian neural orga-
nization, whether marsupial or placental (Smith, 1910;
Loo, 1930; Edinger, 1948; Simpson, 1949, 1959; Olsen,
1959; Frost and Masterton, 1992). This view has further
been supported by fossil and geological evidence showing
the existence of mammals virtually identical in bony
structure to extant Didelphids at least since the Creta-
ceous and perhaps the Jurassic, 100 million or more years
ago (Gregory, 1929; Clemens, 1968, 1977; Marshall et al.,
1987). Furthermore, certain Monodelphid anatomical
characteristics, such as small body size and lack of a
marsupial pouch, suggest that this species may be more
“primitive” and characteristic of the original metatherian
form than Didelphis virginiana (Kirsch, 1977; Marshall,
1979; Reig et al., 1987).
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Somatosensory and motor mapping procedures were
conducted in 12 adult Gray Short-tailed opossums (Mono-
delphis domestica; 7 male, 5 female). Body weights ranged
from 45 to 139 g. Combined somatosensory and motor
mapping procedures were conducted in seven animals. In
addition, exclusive somatosensory mapping (i.e., without
motor mapping procedures) was conducted in one animal,
whereas exclusive motor mapping procedures (i.e., with-
out somatosensory procedures) were conducted in four
animals. Three additional animals were used to examine
the cortical architecture in sections from ﬂattened cortex
processed for myelin.
Surgical and electrophysiological techniques

esthetized to effect with halothane/nitrous oxide. Then
one of three anesthetic protocols was followed during sur-
gical and electrophysiological procedures, depending upon
the particular requirements of the mapping experiment.
For each anesthetic protocol, an initial dose was adminis-
tered to establish a deep anesthetic level, then subsequent
smaller doses were administered to maintain adequate
anesthesia. As the anesthetics were given to effect, and
varied across animals, the doses are approximate. These

Somatosensory mapping procedures. For multi-
unit somatosensory recordings, a glass micropipette ﬁlled
with 3.5 M NaCl (impedance 5 500 kV–1 MV), or a tung-
sten microelectrode (impedance 5 1M V) was introduced
on a ﬁne grid pattern, sited with reference to the surface
vasculature (approximately 250–500 mm interpenetration
distances; Fig. 1). Results obtained with the two micro-
electrode types were identical and are pooled in the re-
mainder of the paper. At each site, the electrode was
advanced perpendicular to the cortical surface to depths
ranging from 400 to 800mm. Minimal cutaneous receptive
ﬁelds were deﬁned by determining the skin ﬁeld over
which cortical neurons were driven by just visible inden-
tation of the skin with a ﬁne glass probe, or by hair
bending. Somatosensory mapping procedures lasted 4 to
12 hours.
Motor mapping procedures. Motor mapping results
were based primarily on intracortical microstimulation
(ICMS) experiments. At each site, ICMS mapping proce-
dures were employed to determine the movement(s)
evoked by stimulation at near-threshold current levels.
Details of this procedure are presented in Nudo et al.
(1992, 1996). Brieﬂy, at each site, a glass micropipette
ﬁlled with 3.5 M NaCl (impedance 5 500 kV–1 MV; out-
side tip diameter 5 10–20 mm) was introduced on the
same grid pattern used for somatosensory mapping. The
electrode was advanced to approximately 1,200 mm, be-
cause movements were evoked at the lowest current levels
at this depth. If motor responses were not evoked at this
depth, other depths ranging from 800 to 1,700 mm were
explored. This range corresponds approximately to layers
4 through 6. The stimulus consisted of 40-msec current
trains of 200-msec cathodal pulses delivered at 350 pulses/
sec from an electrically isolated stimulation circuit. The
maximum current used in these experiments was 60 mA,
though higher currents were used at the conclusion of
each experiment (up to 100 mA) to determine whether
responses could be evoked at previously nonresponsive
sites (n 5 6 animals). In several cases, the train length of
stimulation at sites where no movement could be evoked
was extended to 80 ms.
Two additional stimulation procedures were used in
four animals to determine whether any additional motor
responses could be evoked by using techniques designed to
stimulate larger volumes of cortical tissue, and hence,
larger numbers of cortical neurons: 1) depth stimulation
with low-impedance microelectrodes (hereafter called
“low-impedance depth stimulation”) employed electrodes
with impedances of 100 k V. These electrodes also were
glass micropipettes ﬁlled with 3.5 M NaCl, but outside tip
diameters were larger. The use of these higher impedance
electrodes required signiﬁcantly higher current levels
(maximum 5 500 mA) to evoke movements (n 5 3); 2)
bipolar electrical stimulation of the cortical surface em-
ployed two ﬁne stainless steel wires with a separation of
350–500 mm. These bipolar electrodes were similar to
those used by Lende (1963c) and allow for a more direct
comparison with his earlier study. Maximum current us-
ing bipolar stimulation was 3 mA (n 5 4). Two of the
animals used in bipolar surface stimulation mapping were
also used in low-impedance depth stimulation mapping.
Motor mapping procedures lasted 2 to 12 hours.
At the conclusion of the mapping procedures, reference
marks were placed at the topographic boundaries within
the sensorimotor area either by small electrolytic lesions
or by injection of ﬂuorescent latex microspheres.
Histology
Following electrophysiological procedures, the animals
were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (8
mg/100 g) and perfused transcardially with 0.9% saline
followed by 10% formalin as ﬁxative. The brains were then
transferred to a solution of 30% sucrose formalin for 1–2
weeks. Coronal sections of 50-mm thickness were cut on a
freezing microtome and mounted onto gelatin-coated
slides and alternate sections stained for Nissl substance
with cresyl violet. These sections were used to examine
the cytoarchitecture and to determine the topographic
location and depth of cortical recordings.
Three additional animals that had not undergone neu-
rophysiologic procedures were anesthetized and perfused
in the same manner, and their brains removed and stored
in 5% formalin in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 for 1–8
weeks. The cortex from each hemisphere was separated

RESULTS
Detailed somatosensory maps were derived in ﬁve ani-
mals, ICMS motor maps in ﬁve animals, bipolar stimula-
tion motor maps in four animals, and low-impedance
depth stimulation motor maps in three animals.
Microelectrode mapping of multi-unit-evoked responses
revealed a complete representation of the contralateral
body surface within a large region of midrostral neocortex.
In addition, a second, virtually complete, but smaller
representation was revealed adjacent and lateral to the
larger representation. The second representation was a
mirror image of the ﬁrst representation reversed along the
representation of the glabrous snout.
Microelectrode electrical stimulation of this same region
of neocortex resulted in movements that were conﬁned
strictly to the face. At virtually all sites from which move-
ments could be evoked, electrical stimulation resulted
only in vibrissae movement. These movements were found
to roughly correspond to body parts congruent with the
receptive ﬁelds recorded at each site. Detailed results of
somatosensory and motor mapping experiments are re-
ported below.
Microelectrode mapping of
somatosensory cortex
Microelectrode recordings provided sufﬁcient evidence
for two somatotopically organized areas of cortex, one
located rostromedially and the other caudolaterally, in
four of the ﬁve opossums in which somatosensory mapping
was completed (see Figs. 1, 2; and list of abbreviations). In
the remaining opossum, the spatial resolution of the elec-
trode penetrations was not sufﬁcient to differentiate the
two regions. The relative locations of electrode penetra-
tions where neurons were responsive to light touch on the
body surface or to movement of body hairs are shown for 5
cases in Figures 1 and 2. Area boundaries were estimated
by drawing borders midway between two adjacent record-
ing sites determined to be activated by cutaneous stimu-
lation of different body parts. Sites with receptive ﬁelds of
two or more body parts were often found on the border
between the single body part representations. In each
case, additional recording sites in adjacent regions of cor-
tex were not activated by cutaneous stimuli, and thus
were deﬁned as “nonresponsive.” Boundaries between the
two somatotopically organized body representations were
estimated by a reversal in somatotopic order and an in-
crease in receptive ﬁeld size (as described in detail below)
and are delineated by a dashed line.
Primary somatosensory area (S1). Somatosensory
mapping procedures revealed a complete representation of
the contralateral body surface in which the hindlimb and
tail were represented medially and the face and snout
were represented laterally. This medial-to-lateral se-
quence is similar to the location and somatotopic order of
S1 as observed in other mammals (Kaas, 1983). Figure 1
shows the topographic location of somatosensory record-
ing sites in case 9213 and their location relative to previ-
ously identiﬁed corticospinal somata (Fig. 1, inset; Nudo
and Masterton, 1990a). In this and other cases, the rep-
resentation of the hindlimb and tail was located at or near
the cortical midline. In the majority of cases the primary
somatosensory area contained a relatively large represen-
tation of the distal forelimb lateral to the trunk and hind-
limb representations, and a large representation of the
face and snout, particularly the vibrissae, lateral to the
forelimb representation. Figure 2 shows the topographic
representation of the body surface in four other animals.
In each case the somatotopic representation was located in
a similar medial to lateral and rostral to caudal position in
cortex.
The vast majority of responses were activated by stim-
ulation of the contralateral side of the body. The only
exceptions were at some sites activated by stimulation of
the glabrous snout, midline face, lower jaw (near the mid-
line), and back. Receptive ﬁelds at these sites were often
bilateral.
Figure 3 shows typical receptive ﬁelds from representa-
tive sites in case 9213. In general, the multi-unit receptive
ﬁelds of S1 in Monodelphis were larger in the size of the
body part representation than those seen in primates us-
ing similar techniques (Merzenich et al., 1978; Sur et al.,
1982). Many receptive ﬁelds of the hand extended over
multiple digits, but were usually conﬁned to either the
glabrous or dorsal surface, but not both. Receptive ﬁelds of
the vibrissae typically included several vibrissae and often
entire rows. Receptive ﬁelds of the body were often quite
large, covering the entire trunk and abdomen.
In cases with sufﬁcient detail, a crudely somatotopic
representation of the distal forelimb was consistently ob-
served. In these cases, the ulnar digits of the distal fore-
limb were represented medially and the radial digits were
represented laterally (Fig. 4). Some sites had receptive
ﬁelds conﬁned to single digits. An average of 23% of S1
was devoted to the distal forelimb (see Table 1). In addi-
tion, a crude caudal to rostral somatotopic gradient was
found. That is, wrist and forearm receptive ﬁelds were
always located in the caudal half of the forelimb represen-
tation, whereas digit ﬁelds were more rostral. This ar-
rangement is similar to the topography of the forelimb
representation in other species (see Kaas, 1983 for re-
view).
Somatotopy was also evident in the face representation
across S1 (Fig. 5). First, the lower jaw representation was
typically located rostromedial to the upper jaw representa-
tion (Figs. 5, 6). Second, proceeding in a caudomedial to
rostrolateral direction within the upper or lower jaw repre-
sentation, receptive ﬁelds were found ﬁrst on the posterior
aspect of the jaw and then on progressively more anterior
aspects of the jaw and/or snout and oral cavity (lower jaw:
sites A, and J through M in Fig. 5; upper jaw: sites 1 through
6 and C in Fig. 6). Third, a crude somatotopic arrangement
was observed for the mystacial vibrissae. That is, receptive
ﬁelds for sites progressing in a medial to lateral direction
indicated that the posterior mystacial vibrissae were repre-
sented medial to anterior mystacial vibrissae and snout
(sites B–D in Fig. 5 and sites 1–4 in Fig. 6). A more orderly
somatotopic arrangement of speciﬁc single vibrissae or rows

Electrical stimulation maps of
somatosensory cortex
Electrical stimulation maps of motor outputs from the
physiologically identiﬁed somatosensory cortex were de-
rived by using three different techniques as described in
Materials and Methods: ICMS, low-impedance depth
stimulation, and bipolar surface stimulation. The results
using each of these techniques is described, in turn, below.
Intracortical microstimulation (ICMS). By using
ICMS, sites throughout the entire cortical extent of the
physiologically deﬁned somatosensory cortex were ex-
plored for stimulus-evoked movements of skeletal muscu-
lature. In addition, sites immediately rostral and caudal
to the somatosensory cortex were examined for stimulus-
evoked responses. In most cases, movements were evoked
by stimulation at sites within S1, but at no sites within S2.
One exception was case 9211, where responses were
evoked in more lateral portions of the sensory-response
area that may be S2 (see Fig. 13). In most cases, ICMS
evoked movements of vibrissae only, with an average
threshold of 50 mA and ranging from a low of 9 mAt ot h e
maximum current level of 100 mA. Figure 7 shows the
topographic location of ICMS sites in case 9213 and their
location relative to previously identiﬁed corticospinal so-
mata (Fig. 7, inset; Nudo and Masterton, 1990a). In this
case, ICMS procedures resulted in movement of vibrissae
at 13 sites and movement of the lower jaw at one site. In
each of the other cases, ICMS procedures resulted only in
movement of mystacial and lower jaw vibrissae. In the ﬁve

Nissl stains of coronal sections through somatosensory

DISCUSSION
The present results suggest that somatosensory cortex
in the Monodelphis opossum consists of at least two soma-
totopically organized ﬁelds, S1 and S2, similar in position
and organization to those seen in other mammals. In
contrast to results from other mammals, electrical stimu-
lation mapping did not reveal a complete motor represen-
tation within somatosensory cortex. Virtually all evoked
movements were movements of mystacial or lower jaw
vibrissae. ICMS maps suggest that these evoked re-
sponses are predominantly conﬁned to S1.
The primary somatosensory area (S1)
The position of S1 in Monodelphis is similar to that
observed in Didelphis and other mammalian species
(Lende, 1963b; Beck et al., 1996; see Kaas, 1983; Johnson,
1990). Also, the general somatotopic organization of S1 is
similar to that shown in other mammals, such as Didel-
phis (Lende, 1963b; Pubols et al., 1976; Beck et al., 1996)
rat (Woolsey, 1958; Hall and Lindholm, 1974), shrews
(Catania et al., 1999), cat (Woolsey, 1958; Dykes et al.,
1980; Felleman et al., 1981), insectivores (Catania and
Kaas, 1995; Krubitzer et al., 1997; Pobirsky et al., 1998),
tree shrew (Sur et al., 1980b), prosimians (Krishnamurti
et al., 1976; Sur et al., 1980a), and primates (Merzenich et
al., 1978; Sur et al., 1982; Krubitzer and Kaas, 1990; see
Kaas, 1983 for review). The existence of S1 has also been
documented in several Australian marsupials (Johnson et
al., 1973; Johnson, 1990; Elston et al., 1993; Weller, 1993;
Huffman et al., 1999), with a somatotopic organization
similar to that found in the Didelphid opossums. As in
most other mammals, S1 in Monodelphis contains a com-

The secondary somatosensory area (S2)
The present results provide evidence for a second so-
matosensory representation of the body, positioned lateral
to S1. This body representation is oriented similarly to S2
reported in Didelphis (Lende, 1963b; Pubols, 1977; Beck et
al., 1996), as well as other mammalian species, with a
mirror reversal of the organization found in S1 (Krubitzer
et al., 1986; Johnson, 1990; Rowe, 1990; Krubitzer, 1996).
A secondary somatosensory area has been identiﬁed in
several Australian marsupials, including the brush-tailed
possum (Elston et al., 1993; Coleman et al., 1999) and the
striped opossum (Huffman et al., 1999). In addition to S1,
at least one lateral somatosensory ﬁeld corresponding to
S2 has been identiﬁed in virtually all mammals studied,
suggesting that these ﬁelds were retained from a common
ancestor and ﬁt a basic mammalian plan. In Didelphis, a
third representation responsive to cutaneous stimulation
is found lateral to S1 and rostroventral to S2, and is
believed to be homologous to the parietal ventral area (PV)
in eutherian mammals (Beck et al., 1996; see Krubitzer et
al., 1993 for review). Two additional representations re-
sponsive to stimulation of deep receptors are positioned
immediately rostral and caudal to S1, and referred to as
the rostral and caudal somatosensory areas, SR and SC.
The present results did not reveal the existence of these
additional representations, although they were sought.
The second representation shown here is believed to be a
homologue of S2 in Didelphis because of its orientation
with the distal forelimb lateral and mouth rostral (Fig. 1),
whereas in the PV representation in Didelphis and other
mammals the distal forelimb is medial and the mouth is
caudomedial to the body representation (Krubitzer et al.,
1993; Beck et al., 1996). If theMonodelphis organization is
accepted as the more primitive condition from which Di-
delphis organization is derived (see Introduction), evi-
dence of only two somatosensory ﬁelds in Monodelphis
and a total of ﬁve in Didelphis may support the long-held
belief that multiple ﬁelds represent the differentiation of
new cortical areas from preexisting areas (Brodmann,
1909; von Economo, 1929; Kaas, 1987, 1989).
A PV representation and two additional rostral ﬁelds
responsive to stimulation of deep receptors are seen in
present-day monotremes, the short-billed echidna, and
platypus (Krubitzer et al., 1995). The presence of a PV
representation in monotremes that diverged from therian
mammals over 130 million years ago (before the appear-
ance of Didelphids; Clemens, 1989; Flannery, 1989; Wes-
terman and Edwards, 1992) and apparent lack of a PV
representation in Monodelphis suggests that PV (as well
as additional deep receptor ﬁelds) may have evolved inde-
pendently in monotremes and Didelphis. Alternatively,
it is possible that PV is vestigial in Monodelphis. The
present results cannot adequately resolve this issue, be-
cause the spatial resolution of the present maps was rel-
atively low in the most ventral aspects of the explored
region. Higher resolution maps in this region may yet
reveal a third somatosensory area in this species.

vealing, because they were identical to those used by
Lende to show a complete sensorimotor amalgam in Di-
delphis opossums (Lende, 1963a,c). These relatively more
crude techniques result in stimulation of a much greater

more reﬁned movements of the face. This pressure may

representation rostral to S1 was established. Similar to

this large vibrissae motor representation may be related
to the behavioral importance of the area in Monodelphis.

It has been suggested that vibrissae are among the
phylogenetically youngest of the specialized mammalian
peripheral sensory organs, lacking in monotremes and
emerging in theria about 120 million years ago (Pocock,
1914; Brecht et al., 1997). Although most studies of the
vibrissae have examined their sensory roles in behavior, it
is clear that mystacial vibrissae are active during ex-
ploratory and discriminative behaviors, at least in rats
(Welker, 1964; Carvell and Simons, 1990, 1995; Bermejo
et al., 1996; Kleinfeld et al., 1999). Results from behav-
ioral studies in the rat suggest that the large mystacial
vibrissae (macrovibrissae) are involved in spatial percep-
tion (Hutson and Masterton, 1986) and act as distance
decoders (Brecht et al., 1997). Any advantage that reﬁned
movement of the vibrissae could provide to an individual
would have contributed to the evolutionary pressures for
their cortical motor representation. Furthermore, it has
been suggested that the smaller vibrissae (microvibrissae)
on the rostroventral portion of the snout constitute an
object-recognition organ, possessing a large receptor density
crucial for tactile object recognition (Brecht et al., 1997). This
view ﬁts well with the ﬁndings that the rostroventral snout
is important in nuzzling behavior (Poran et al., 1993a).
It has long been established that movement can be
evoked from electrical stimulation of S1 in primates
(Schafer, 1900; Penﬁeld and Boldrey, 1937; Penﬁeld and
Rasmussen, 1952; Woolsey et al., 1953; Woolsey, 1958)
and cats (Woolsey, 1947). Similarly, Malis et al. (1953)
found that sensory-evoked potentials could be elicited in
precentral motor cortex in monkeys. More recent electro-
physiological studies have conﬁrmed somatic motor func-
tions in S1 (Doetsch and Gardner, 1972; Sapienza et al.,
1981; Gioanni and Lamarche, 1985) and somatic sensory
functions in M1 (Welt et al., 1967; Thompson et al., 1970;
Rose´n and Asanuma, 1972; Wiesendanger, 1973;Asanuma
et al., 1979, 1980; Tanji and Wise, 1981; Strick and Pres-
ton, 1982; Asanuma and Arissian, 1984; Nudo et al.,
1997).
In general, electrically evoked motor responses in S1
resemble those evoked in M1, with the exception that
stimulation thresholds are greater (Welker et al., 1957;
Woolsey, 1958; Doetsch and Gardner, 1972; Sessle and
Weisendanger, 1982). In both the results presented here
and results reported in electrical stimulation studies of
cortex in Didelphis (Lende, 1963c; Beck et al., 1996),
movements were most often evoked by using relatively
high threshold stimulation; higher than the , 20 mA typ-
ically observed in microstimulation studies of M1 of pri-
mates (Sessle and Weisendanger, 1982; Stepniewska et
al., 1993; Nudo et al., 1992, 1996). This disparity in
thresholds for evoked movements suggests that the elec-
trically evoked motor responses observed in Monodelphis
are representative of the motor component of somatosen-
sory cortex found in other mammals.
Laterality of responses. ICMS and unit recordings in
a macaque monkey have shown that ipsilateral and bilat-
eral movements of the hand are represented anterolateral
to regions associated with the more common contralateral
movements (Aizawa et al., 1990). Functional magnetic
resonance imaging in humans has shown that segregated
ipsilateral response areas are anterolateral to contralat-
eral response areas (Cramer et al., 1999). That ICMS
ipsilateral and bilateral vibrissae zones are segregated
similarly in Monodelphis may suggest that this organ-
ization developed early in the appearance of motor-
responsive areas of cortex and has been retained through
the evolution of primate motor cortex.
It is unclear why a consistent segregation of response
zones was not observed in low-impedance or depth stim-
ulation mapping. The most complete low-impedance stim-
ulation case had predominantly ipsilateral vibrissae re-
sponses (Fig. 9) and may have been an anomalous animal.
Input-output relationships. The ﬁnding that vibris-
sae motor response sites typically overlapped sites with
sensory receptive ﬁelds of the vibrissae and other parts of
the face is roughly similar to that found in somatomotor
cortex of other mammals. Cortical sites receiving tactile
input from particular parts of the face, hand, or trunk in
monkeys roughly overlap sites that result in movements
in closely related parts of the body upon stimulation
(Woolsey, 1958; Doetsch and Gardner, 1972). A similar
rough congruence of sensory input and motor output is
seen in sensorimotor cortex of rats (Sapienza et al., 1981)
cats (Asanuma et al., 1968), and primates (Rose ´n and
Asanuma, 1972; Sessle and Weisendanger, 1982; Strick
and Preston, 1982).

Evolution of sensorimotor cortex
Lende’s view that S1 of Didelphis is an “amalgam” of S1
and M1 suggests that separate S1 and M1 areas evolved in
placental mammals from a single area possessing both
sensory and motor functions. More recent studies suggest
that motor responses evoked upon stimulation of S1 are
similar to the higher threshold S1 motor responses com-
monly seen in other mammals (Beck et al., 1996). Beck et
al. (1996) found that even the few sites rostral to the
sensory representation where movements were evoked
required higher thresholds than one would expect in an
M1 representation in eutherian mammals (Neafsey et al.,
1986; Sessle and Weisendanger, 1982; Stepniewska et al.,
1993). These results suggest that the amalgam proposed
by Lende (1963a) might have been a reﬂection of motor
components within the S1 representation, similar to that
found in the S1 of other mammals. The electrophysiolog-
ical results presented here in Monodelphis suggest that
this species has a motor component within S1, though it is
not as well-developed (i.e., complete) as that observed in
other mammals, and not as developed as that seen in
Didelphis. This result may represent a primordial S1 con-
dition, in which a complete somatosensory map has been
achieved and retained, but the motor components of S1
have not yet fully developed. In this primitive condition,
the anatomical substrate required for cortical control of
movements below the level of the face is not present.
The cytoarchitecture of the vibrissae motor response
area in S1 of Monodelphis also suggests that this is a
sensory area rather than a true motor cortex. The vibris-
sae response area has a notable granular layer (layer IV)
and does not have the agranular appearance notable of
true motor cortex (Fig. 15; and see Gray, 1924; Walsh and
Ebner, 1970 in Didelphis). In addition, the myelin stain-
ing pattern is similar to that shown for somatosensory
cortex of other mammals (see Krubitzer, 1995).
Sensorimotor cortex in Monodelphis appears to be the
least developed in any mammal studied thus far, and may
represent the primordial condition from which primate
sensory and motor cortex arose. Figure 17 illustrates a
hypothetical model of evolutionary divergence of sensori-
motor cortex supported by the results reported here. Mo-
nodelphis has a complete S1 representation, as well as a
second, S2 representation, with an incomplete motor com-
ponent overlapping the S1 representation of the face
(grade 1). Didelphis has a complete S1 representation (as
well as several additional representations (Beck et al.,
1996) with a complete motor representation overlapping
the somatosensory representation in S1 (grade 2; Lende,
1963a). This S1 motor representation in Didelphis is not
unlike that found in S1 of placental mammals, as high-
threshold stimulation is required to evoke movement. A
true motor cortex, as evidenced by a reversed motor rep-
resentation rostral to the somatosensory representation
and movements evoked at relatively low stimulating cur-
rents, is apparent in rats (grade 3). In this grade, the
separation of S1 and M1 is not complete, because an
overlap has been demonstrated over most of the hindlimb
representation and part of the forelimb representation
(Hall and Lindholm, 1974; Donoghue and Wise, 1982;
Sanderson et al., 1983). A complete and separate motor
cortex, as evidenced by a reversed motor representation
rostral to the somatosensory representation and low-
threshold evoked movement, is apparent in primates
(grade 4).
The cortical organization in monotremes, however, sug-
gests that sensorimotor differentiation may not have fol-
lowed this simple linear progression. For example, by
using surface stimulation techniques in the prototherian
echidna, Lende (1964) found a complete motor represen-
tation overlapping the somatosensory representation. By
using surface stimulation techniques in the platypus, Bo-
hringer and Rowe (1977) found a partial overlap of the
motor and somatosensory representations. Electrical
stimulation evoked movements of the bill and forelimb,
but not the hindlimb. Thus, it would appear that the
motor component of somatosensory cortex is more com-
plete in monotremes than in Monodelphis opossums.
Furthermore, an area rostral to somatosensory cortex in
monotremes is in some ways characteristic of motor cor-
tex. In early surface stimulation studies in echidna (Abbie,
1938; Goldby, 1939), evoked movements were observed in
an area rostral to sulcus a, and thus rostral to S1 (Kru-
bitzer et al., 1995). Surface stimulation studies in platy-
pus also resulted in evoked movements rostral to S1,
including representations of the forelimb. Based on elec-
trophysiogical and cytoarchitectonic criteria, two ﬁelds
rostral to S1 have been identiﬁed in both echidna and
platypus (Krubitzer et al., 1995). The rostral ﬁeld (ﬁeld R)
contains neurons responsive to stimulation of deep recep-
tors, whereas the manipulation ﬁeld (ﬁeld M) has the
architectonic appearance of the eutherian M1 (Ulinski,
1984; Krubitzer et al., 1995).
These ﬁndings of a more complete motor component in
somatosensory cortex and a possible M1 homologue ros-
tral to S1 suggest that a more developed sensorimotor
area may have existed in mammals previous to the emer-
gence of Didelphids. If so, then sensorimotor cortex in
Monodelphis may be the result of secondary simpliﬁ-
cation. Alternatively, the forelimb component of S1 in
monotremes may be homoplaseous to that of Didelphis,
having arisen through convergent evolution. Similarly,
the rostral motor area in monotremes may be homo-
plaseous to M1 in eutherian mammals.

Although the interpretation of these results in mono-
tremes remains speculative, it is now clear that the opos-
sum’s “sensorimotor amalgam” is not motor cortex in the
same sense as it is in grade 3 and 4 mammals. The somato-
sensory cortex of grade 3 and 4 mammals is not without
some motor properties, as mentioned above. But a motor
component in the opossum’s sensorimotor area is not, by
itself, sufﬁcient grounds to consider it motor cortex. Corre-
spondence of sensory and motor maps in the opossum’s sen-
sorimotor area is just what is seen in grade 3 and 4 mam-
mals. Therefore, the sensorimotor “amalgam” of opossum
cortex is probably no more “motor” than the sensory cortex
of, for example, monkey. In addition to the differentiation of
somatosensory ﬁelds, as has been reported in a number of
recent papers, it would appear that the motor component of
this predominantly somatosensory cortical ﬁeld developed in
stages. In addition, it is probably safe to suggest that in
opossum cortex speciﬁcally and in primitive, or grade one
and two cortex generally, there is no true motor cortex. If
this is the case (and opossums provide a reasonable repre-
sentation of the primitive conditions), then motor cortex
must have arisen anew sometime later in evolution. It is not
entirely clear when true motor cortex might have emerged.
This riddle may be difﬁcult to solve, because representatives
of intermediate conditions may not exist among extant mam-
mals. However, as evolutionary pressures for greater dexter-
ity of the distal skeletal musculature grew, augmentations of
the corticospinal tract from a primitive to a more advanced
pathway seem to have paralleled the origin of a true motor
cortex.
