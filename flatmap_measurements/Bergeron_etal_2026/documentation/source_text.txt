Abstract
Due to its large gyrencephalic brain, the pig is increasingly used for neuroscience research, especially for the preclinical testing
of novel neuroprostheses. However, our understanding of the pig ’s motor system remains limited compared with the common
species used for neuroscience research. Here, we aimed to characterize the forelimb and hindlimb representation of the pig
motor cortex using intracortical microstimulation (ICMS). Three domestic pigs ( Sus scrofa ) were placed in a modi ﬁed stereotactic
frame and maintained under intravenous propofol sedation. We mapped the motor cortex using ICMS, applied at varying cortical
coordinates and depths. For each site, we recorded the electrode depth eliciting the maximal limb response and determined the
motor threshold. Responses were assessed visually and via electromyographic recordings. ICMS uncovered a large forelimb rep-
resentation, with stereotypical contralateral responses. Conversely, the hindlimb representation was smaller and located within
the interhemispheric ﬁssure. The mean threshold of the ﬁve most responsive forelimb sites was 75 ± 25 μA, compared with
280 ± 45 μA for hindlimb sites ( P < 0.01). A summation of stimulations in the hindlimb representation of the motor cortex unilater-
ally triggered bilateral alternating hindlimb movements. These results suggest that while the porcine cortex can directly com-
mand forelimb movements via the corticospinal pathway, cortical control of hindlimb likely relies on polysynaptic pathways
through the brainstem, such as the corticoreticulospinal pathway.

INTRODUCTION
Rodents and nonhuman primates have long been the pre-
dominant animal models in neuroscience. The pig ( Sus
scrofa) is increasingly recognized as a valuable preclinical
model for neuroscience research and neuroprosthesis bio-
compatibility testing ( 1, 2). The gyrencephalic pig brain
shares closer anatomical ( 3, 4) and developmental ( 5) simi-
larities with the human brain than commonly used small
laboratory animals and has long been used in other medical
ﬁelds, such as cardiology and transplantation, due to their
similarity to humans in anatomical size and structure, physi-
ology, and immunology (6).
To date, pigs have been used in preclinical studies for
various neuroprosthetic devices, including deep brain
stimulation (DBS) ( 7–9), vagus nerve stimulation ( 10), epi-
d u r a ls p i n a lc o r ds t i m u l a t i o n(11, 12), intradural spinal
cord stimulation ( 13–18), and chronic cortical recordings
with electrocorticographic ( 19) and micro-electrode arrays
(20). Notably, pigs serve as the main preclinical model for
biocompatibility testing of novel brain-machine interfa-
ces, used by companies like Neuralink (Austin) ( 21)a n d
Precision Medicine (New York) ( 22). Porcine models have
also been developed for a range of nervous system disor-
ders, including stroke ( 23, 24) and spinal cord injury (SCI)
(25–30).

Building on our previous work in rodent and feline mod-
els, where we developed a neuroprosthesis delivering
intracortical microstimulation (ICMS) in synchrony with
locomotion to modulate movements and improve motor
recovery after SCI ( 31–35), we aimed to test the feasibility of
studying cortical neuroprostheses on that model. Although
the functional anatomy of the porcine spinal cord and
nerve roots is well characterized ( 36, 37), the cortical repre-
sentation of the pig hindlimb remains poorly de ﬁned.
Transcranial electrical stimulation can generate hindlimb
movements ( 38, 39), but less robustly than forelimb
responses. The only prior ICMS studies in pigs date back to
1966 ( 40)a n d1 9 8 6( 41), with minimal description of the
hindlimb representation. Moreover, tracing studies sug-
gest that the porcine corticospinal tract does not extend
beyond the cervico-thoracic levels ( 42–44), implying that
hindlimb responses to cortical stimulation may rely pre-
dominantly on polysynaptic pathways.
In this study, we used ICMS to derive cortical motor
maps in three anesthetized pigs, enabling a detailed char-
acterization of both the hindlimb and forelimb motor
representations.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Animals
All procedures adhered to the Canadian Council on
Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the Comit /C19e
d’/C19Ethique et de l ’Exp/C19erimentation Animale (CEEA; animal
ethics committee) at Hôpital du Sacr /C19e-Coeur de Montr /C19eal.
Experiments were conducted on domestic pigs ( Sus scrofa )
purchased from Ferme G/C19erard Riopel. Three female pigs (2 –
2.5 mo old, weighing 21, 23, and 24 kg) were used. Animals
were housed individually with constant access to water and
were fed three times daily.
Intracortical Microstimulation
Animals were preanesthetized with intramuscular injec-
tions of ketamine (20 mg/kg), xylazine (2 mg/kg), buprenor-
phine (0.05 mg/kg), acepromazine (0.05 mg/kg), and
atropine (0.05 mg/kg). After intubation, pigs were placed
under assisted ventilation and maintained on a heating mat
to maintain body temperature above 35 /C14 C. The head was
secured in a modiﬁed stereotactic frame using external audi-
tory canal bars and a hard palate plate. The torso was ele-
vated to allow unrestricted motion of the forelimbs and
hindlimbs. Anesthesia was maintained with intravenous
propofol (10 mg/mL, 10 –30 mL/h), adjusted to preserve
evoked ICMS responses. Optimal anesthesia was character-
ized by mild spontaneous limb twitching; lighter anesthesia
(tachycardia or increased twitching) was managed with pro-
pofol boluses. Electrocardiogram (EKG) monitoring was con-
tinuous. The experimental setting is summarized in Fig. 1A.
A large craniotomy was performed bilaterally to expose the
motor cortex and surrounding gyri, spanning 2-cm posterior
and 3-cm anterior to the coronal suture, and /C24 3 cm lateral to
the sagittal suture. The dura was opened in a “C” shape and
reﬂected toward the midline to preserve the superior sagittal
sinus. If necessary, cortical veins adhering to the dura were
coagulated and cut to facilitate proper reﬂection of the dura.
The cortical surface was irrigated with saline heated to body
temperature. The motor cortex was localized bilaterally
between the sulcus ansatus posterolaterally and the sulcus
cruciatus anteromedially (3, 40)( Fig. 1B). A micromanipula-
tor was used to determine the coordinates of the motor cor-
tex boundaries, and a grid of 1-mm squares was overlaid to
guide electrode penetrations. Glass-coated platinum/irid-
ium microelectrodes with an input impedance of 0.5 ± 0.1
MX (1,000 Hz, 10 nA) were used (FHC). Electrodes were
advanced into the neocortex to a depth of 0.5 mm using a
microdrive (David Kopf Instruments model 2650), with
0.5-mm increments until the area of peak responses was
determined. An isolated pulse stimulator was used to
deliver electrical current (Model 2100, A-M Systems). A
ground stimulation lead was placed in contact with the
neck muscles. Stimulation trains consisted of 13 biphasic
cathodal-lead pulses, each with a 200- μsd u r a t i o na taf r e -
quency of 333 Hz, with a train rate of 1 Hz. Current inten-
sity was gradually increased from 0 μA to 1 mA maximum.
Threshold was de ﬁned as the lowest intensity consistently
producing a movement. Sites unresponsive at 1 mA were
considered nonresponsive. We sampled each coordinate at
multiple depths to identify the depths evoking motor
responses at the lowest amplitude. Throughout the sur-
gery, anesthetic levels were monitored by verifying evoked
movements at previously de ﬁned positive-response sites
and assessing the borders of nonresponsive points. To
assess motor responses, we used electromyography (tibi-
alis anterior and vastus lateralis bilaterally) and high
frame-rate video recording (120 Hz, Teledyne FLIR) for vis-
ual analysis of evoked movements. A light-emitting diode
synchronized with stimulator output was ﬁxed to the ste-
reotaxic frame in the camera ﬁeld-of-view. Motor maps
were generated from positive-response sites, with each
site representing 1 mm
2. The hindlimb representation was
the primary focus; forelimb mapping was partial, and
facial (corticobulbar) representation was only broadly out-
lined. We performed ICMS bilaterally in three pigs in total,
systematically applying penetrations 1 mm apart across
the motor cortex. At each site, stimulations were delivered
at depths from 1 to 7 mm in 1-mm increments, resulting in
/C24 700 stimulation sites. At the end of the experiment, ani-
mals were deeply anesthetized with iso ﬂurane and eutha-
nized with intravenous KCl. Brains were extracted, postﬁxed
with 16% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 1 mo, then
cryoprotected in a solution of 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PBS for 1
wk. The brain was frozen, sectioned around the motor cor-
tex, and cut into 40- lm thick sagittal sections (3 hemi-
spheres) and coronal sections (3 hemispheres) using a
cryostat. The sections were stained with Luxol Fast Blue
(0.1%, Sigma, S3382) and Cresyl Violet (0.5%, Alfa Aesar,
J64318). Bright- ﬁeld microscopy images were taken at /C2 4
magniﬁcation (Olympus BX63).

RESULTS
Intracortical Microstimulation
By applying ICMS bilaterally in three pigs (more than
700 stimulation sites), we determined the minimal current
required to evoke a visible limb movement, identi ﬁed the
depth of maximal response, and derived a somatotopic
motor map ( Fig. 2 , Supplemental Video S1). The sites evok-
ing maximal hindlimb motor responses were at the medial
edge of the motor cortex, whereas sites evoking a predomi-
nant forelimb response occupied a larger area of the grid
centrally and laterally ( Fig. 2, A and B); stimulation of the
lateral and superior aspect of forelimb motor map evoked
various facial responses, including the lips, tongue, and
neck ( Fig. 2, A and B). As we focused on cortical limb rep-
resentation, we did not speci ﬁcally map corticobulbar out-
puts in this study. The histological analysis of electrode
tracts allowed to locate the layer V of the motor cortex at
around 2 mm from the cortical surface ( Fig. 2 C). Electrode
penetrations can cause a slight depression of the cortex,
leading to a mild overestimation of penetration depths. As
such, responses obtained between 2 and 4 mm of depths
were considered cortical responses and electrode penetra-
tions at depths of 5 –7 mm were considered as white-matter
responses (within the corona radiata) (Fig. 2A). At some coor-
dinates, forelimb movements were often maximally evoked
superﬁcially (2 –3 mm depths, corresponding to layer V),
whereas hindlimb movements were elicited deeper down
(6–7 mm depths, presumably stimulating white-matterﬁbers
from the medial aspect of the cortex joining the corona radi-
ata; Fig. 2, B and C). Taking these ﬁndings into considera-
tion, we adjusted our approach for the ICMS mapping
performed on the third pig, limiting penetration depth to
4 mm, except in our most medial penetrations adjacent to
the interhemispheric ﬁssure (to more precisely map the
medial cortical surface). Electrode penetrations at the medial
margin of the motor cortex (along the interhemispheric ﬁs-
sure) were used to map the medial aspect of the motor cortex
(Fig. 2D, unfolded map of the pig’s motor cortex).
Hindlimb Movements Generated by ICMS
Hindlimb responses were generally subtler than forelimb
responses when using the same stimulation amplitude. At
forelimb sites, robust movements were obtained with cur-

typically required >300 μA( Fig. 2 B). The mean amplitude
threshold of the ﬁve most responsive forelimb sites was
75 ± 25 μA, compared with 280 ± 45 μA for the hindlimb.
Overall recorded forelimb and hindlimb sites, amplitude
thresholds were lower for the forelimb (197 ± 115μA) than the
hindlimb (440 ± 171 μA; P < 0.001). Hindlimb responses
often required repeated stimulations and were less consist-
ent across trials, in contrast to the reproducible contralateral
forelimb responses. Lateralization of responses also differed
between forelimb and hindlimb: forelimb responses were
strictly contralateral, whereas hindlimb responses were fre-
quently ipsilateral or bilateral. Most hindlimb responses
consisted of small twitches along the entire leg, often with
as u b t l e ﬂexion of the contralateral hooves or fetlock
(Supplemental Video S1). ICMS applied to the medial portion
of the motor cortex often triggered shoulder movements that

DISCUSSION
In this study, we characterized the forelimb and hindlimb
representations of the pig motor cortex. We found that the
hindlimb motor representation is relatively small compared
with the forelimb, and that movements evoked by cortical
stimulation were more consistent and pronounced in the
face and forelimb than in the hindlimbs. Our results align
with previous ICMS studies in pigs. In Breazile et al. ( 40), a
summation of 3–4 monophasic square-wave stimuli of 4 ms,
7–10 V in the medial-caudal region of the motor cortex could
elicit isolated hindlimb movements, a region corresponding
to the primary somatosensory hindlimb representation,
which is on the medial surface of the cruciate gyrus, buried
within the interhemispheric ﬁssure ( 46). The discrepancy
between the forelimb and hindlimb representation in the
motor cortex can be explained by the anatomy of the cortico-
spinal tract in pigs. Complete resection of the pig ’s motor
cortex does not signi ﬁcantly impair locomotion ( 41), and
large cortical and subcortical strokes (middle cerebral artery
occlusion) only produce subtle impairments in gait and fore-
limb movements during locomotion ( 23). In various tracing
studies, the corticospinal tract was shown to reach the cervi-
cal level, but never below the thoracic level ( 42, 44).

the hindlimb motor cortex elicited alternating bursts of

movements. This pattern of alternating activity resembles
the hindlimb muscle activity elicited by repeated stimula-
tion of the medullary reticular formation during ﬁctive
locomotion ( 50–52). In our study, stimulation was applied
to the motor cortex rather than the reticular formation.
Despite this, we hypothesize that stimulating the hindlimb
representation of the pig motor cortex triggers hindlimb
movements via the cortico-reticulo-spinal pathway. Unlike
ﬁctive locomotion studies such as Perreault et al. ( 50), we
did not abolish phasic afferent feedback from the limbs.
Consequently, we could not isolate the speci ﬁc effects of
repeated motor cortex stimulation on locomotor frequency
as cleanly. Nevertheless, our hypothesis of a cortico-retic-
ulo-spinal pathway for limb control in pigs is further sup-
ported by neuroanatomical tracing studies; pig ’sm o t o r
cortex neurons project to the contralateral cervico-thoracic
anterior horn, ipsilateral mesencephalon, and bilateral
medulla oblongata; no monosynaptic projection from the
motor cortex to the lumbar region was ever described ( 42,
44). This hypothesis is also supported by the higher ampli-
tude threshold required to activate the hindlimbs (300 –500
μA) and the temporal summation needed to obtain clear
responses. Forelimb responses were consistent from the
ﬁrst stimulation, at lower amplitude thresholds ( <100 μA),
more consistent with the ICMS literature. The reticular for-
mation is a site of convergence of bilateral cortical inputs
across species ( 53, 54). Taken together, these ﬁndings sug-
gest that the porcine cerebral cortex can directly control
forelimb movements through direct connections to cervical
spinal neurons, but relies primarily on brainstem nuclei to
modulate hindlimb activity.
This study has limitations. First, while the use of the
micromanipulator was ideal for mapping the superior sur-
face of the cortex, mapping the medial surface of the motor
cortex, located in the interhemispheric ﬁssure, proved more
difﬁcult. Because we preserved the midline dura to prevent
hemorrhaging from the superior sagittal sinus, our direct vis-
ualization of the medial aspect was restricted. Consequently,
we had to infer the functional representation of the medial
motor cortex by analyzing the speci ﬁc depths of our most
medial electrode penetrations. Second, we measured ﬂexor
muscles in the hindlimbs (tibialis anterior), but signal from
extensor muscles was too weak and artifacted to properly
measure ﬂexor-extensor alternation during alternated hind-
limb movements. Third, the young age (2 mo) of the pigs
used in the ICMS experiments may limit the generalization
of our results to adult pigs, as the thickness of the motor cor-
tex increases with age, accompanied by changes in cell den-
sity due to myelination and dendritic development ( 5).
Despite these limitations, our study provides an updated
characterization of the pig motor cortex and its descending
pathways, informing the design and interpretation of trans-
lational neurotechnology studies.
